<div id="spenza-merged-article" class="mh-article">
<p>Before you sign an MVNE contract, check ten things: whether the OSS/BSS is cloud-native and event-driven, how it handles eSIM provisioning (SGP.22 and SGP.32), whether it manages several operators and IMSIs, how it charges and rates usage, how it reconciles wholesale costs, whether its APIs follow open standards, how it isolates accounts and entities, whether you can export your data, what the service levels cover, and what leaving would involve. Then prove each answer with a test on your own service instead of accepting a demo.</p>
<p>This checklist is written for teams launching in 2026 and later, especially device makers, enterprises with IoT or mobility needs, and connectivity resellers. It is built around what an operator-neutral platform has to do when connectivity is software: provision, charge, reconcile and report across networks without batch files and manual fixes. It also adds the parts that decide most RFPs: a scoring method, acceptance tests, the commercial questions and the exit check.</p>
<p><em>Last verified 30 October 2026.</em></p>
<h2 id="what-to-check">The ten checks at a glance</h2>
<p>Use this list as the first pass. Each item is expanded in the sections below.</p>
<ol><li><strong>Architecture:</strong> cloud-native, modular services, not a hosted monolith.</li><li><strong>Processing:</strong> real-time, event-driven provisioning and charging instead of overnight files.</li><li><strong>eSIM:</strong> SGP.22 for consumer devices and SGP.32 for IoT, in one provisioning framework.</li><li><strong>Operators:</strong> several networks and multiple IMSIs managed under policy, including agreements you already hold.</li><li><strong>Charging:</strong> event, session and policy-based rating, not only aggregate usage.</li><li><strong>Reconciliation:</strong> automated checks of usage against wholesale cost, with discrepancies flagged quickly.</li><li><strong>Integration:</strong> documented APIs, ideally aligned with TM Forum Open APIs.</li><li><strong>Structure:</strong> multiple brands, legal entities and payer hierarchies on one platform.</li><li><strong>Data:</strong> full export of usage, financial and operational records in documented formats.</li><li><strong>Exit and terms:</strong> service levels, pricing units and a transition path you can verify.</li></ol>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Where this fits.</strong> For the plain definitions of OSS and BSS, read the <a href="/telecom/oss-bss/">OSS and BSS guide</a>. For who does what between MNO, MVNO, MVNE and MVNA, see the <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">roles guide</a>. This page assumes both and concentrates on how to evaluate a provider.</p></div>
<h2 id="who-this-is-for">Who should use this checklist</h2>
<ul><li>Device manufacturers and OEMs embedding connectivity in a product.</li><li>Enterprises deploying IoT or mobility services across countries.</li><li>Connectivity resellers and new MVNO brands choosing a platform partner.</li><li>Product, operations and platform teams comparing MVNE proposals.</li><li>Technical leaders who own the OSS/BSS architecture decision.</li></ul>
<p>If you have not yet decided between operating models, start with the <a href="/mvno/mvno-types/">MVNO types</a> comparison, then return here to evaluate partners.</p>
<h2 id="why-legacy-breaks">Why legacy OSS/BSS architectures break down</h2>
<p>Traditional telecom stacks separated the network-facing OSS from the commercial BSS and joined them with batch interfaces and manual reconciliation. That worked for one operator serving one market. It struggles with the operating models many MVNOs now plan: multi-operator connectivity across countries, large fleets of IoT endpoints, hybrid consumer and enterprise billing, and connectivity bundled with hardware or software.</p>
<p>In those models, one event can require several coordinated actions. A device that roams, switches network or crosses a policy threshold may need a provisioning change, a different rate, a tax treatment and an entry in a report. When each of those happens in a separate system on a different schedule, you get latency, errors and margin you cannot see until the cycle closes.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>A practical test.</strong> If a provider relies on overnight usage files, manual SIM lifecycle interventions or post-cycle margin analysis, it is not built for multi-operator, enterprise or IoT operation. It may still suit a simple single-network consumer launch. Decide which one you are before you compare.</p></div>

<h2 id="service-brief">Start with a service brief and mandatory gates</h2>
<p>A checklist only helps when every provider answers the same question. Before you send it, write a one-page service brief: target customers, countries, devices, whether you need voice, messaging or data, sales channels, account hierarchy and the systems you will keep. Send the same brief to every candidate, including your existing supplier, so the answers describe the same job.</p>
<p>Then split your requirements in two. <strong>Mandatory gates</strong> are what the service cannot launch without, such as access to a particular network, an eSIM journey on your device range, or billing for your payer structure. <strong>Preferences</strong> are everything you would like but could add later. Score preferences. Treat gates as pass or fail. An attractive analytics feature cannot offset a missing network, device or billing workflow.</p>
<h2 id="provisioning">Provisioning and eSIM lifecycle</h2>
<p>Provisioning decides customer experience, cost control and how far operations can scale. A platform should treat it as a programmable service, not a back-office queue.</p>
<h3 id="sgp32">SGP.22 and SGP.32 support</h3>
<p>SGP.22 is the GSMA specification for consumer eSIM remote provisioning. SGP.32 is the GSMA eSIM IoT specification, which splits the on-device assistant into an IoT Profile Assistant (IPA) and adds a server-side eSIM IoT Remote Manager (eIM) so devices without a user interface can have profiles managed remotely. The current version is published by the GSMA as <a href="https://gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/SGP.32-v1.2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SGP.32 v1.2</a>.</p>
<p>Ask whether the platform supports SGP.32 today or on a roadmap, and treat a roadmap as an open item rather than a yes. Ask how consumer and IoT eSIM sit in one provisioning framework, because separate stacks create separate operating teams. Then ask for a live walk-through on the device types you plan to ship: purchase, profile delivery, installation, activation and recovery after an interrupted step. Device support, profile availability and network activation are separate checks, even when the customer sees one journey.</p>
<h3 id="multi-imsi">Multi-operator and multi-IMSI management</h3>
<p>Can the platform hold several operator profiles or IMSIs per device and switch between them by policy, for cost, coverage or performance, without someone doing it by hand? Ask what triggers a switch, who can override it and how the change appears in billing and reporting. Multi-IMSI and eUICC are related but different: multi-IMSI gives a SIM more than one operator identity, while an eUICC lets profiles be downloaded and swapped over the air. Which one helps more depends on the markets and devices in your brief.</p>
<h3 id="zero-touch">Zero-touch activation and lifecycle</h3>
<p>Look for API-driven activation, suspension, plan change and profile management that work without a person in the loop. Manual provisioning does not scale for enterprise or IoT fleets, and every manual step is a place for state to drift between the platform and the network.</p>
<h2 id="charging">Real-time monetization and charging</h2>
<p>Billing for enterprise, IoT and multi-operator services needs more granular models than a monthly plan with a data allowance. Separate three functions that sales material often blurs: charging (rating usage as it happens), billing (turning charges into invoices) and payment (collecting money). The <a href="/mvno/mvno-billing-platforms/">MVNO billing platform guide</a> covers those boundaries in detail. For this checklist, ask four things.</p>
<ul><li><strong>5G Standalone charging.</strong> Does the converged charging function support the 5G SA use cases you plan, such as differentiated quality of service or network slicing, or is that outside the contract?</li><li><strong>Event and session rating.</strong> Can it rate discrete events, sessions or policy actions, not only aggregate usage? Satellite fallback, secure tunnels and bursty IoT traffic need this.</li><li><strong>Hybrid payment structures.</strong> Can it split billing, share payment between parties and issue multi-party invoices? Payer, administrator and line user are often three different parties.</li><li><strong>Anomaly detection.</strong> Does it watch usage in near real time and flag spikes before wholesale cost escalates? Ask what it detects, how fast, and who is alerted.</li></ul>
<h2 id="reconciliation">Reconciliation, revenue assurance and visibility</h2>
<p>As footprints grow, visibility becomes a control. Ask whether the platform automatically reconciles usage and cost across operators and whether discrepancies surface immediately or weeks later. Margin that is only visible after the cycle closes is margin you cannot protect.</p>
<p>Ask also for a single operational view across consumer SIMs, enterprise mobility plans, IoT subscriptions and travel eSIMs. Fragmented tools slow decisions and blur accountability. Where you run more than one brand, region or legal entity, confirm multi-entity support so each can have its own catalog, branding and invoices on shared infrastructure.</p>
<h2 id="questions">Ten questions to ask any MVNE</h2>
<p>Feature lists do not show architectural maturity. These questions do. Each includes what a useful answer looks like.</p>
<ol><li><strong>Is your OSS/BSS cloud-native, or a hosted legacy system?</strong> Look for modular services that scale independently and change without a full release. A hosted monolith can still work, but ask what a pricing or operator change costs in time.</li><li><strong>How do you handle SGP.22 and SGP.32 in one provisioning framework?</strong> Separate consumer and IoT stacks create separate operations. Ask for the roadmap in writing if either is not live.</li><li><strong>Can you support Local Breakout for latency-sensitive enterprise traffic?</strong> Local breakout routes traffic in the visited network instead of back to a home network. Ask which networks and markets support it for your use case.</li><li><strong>What tax, compliance and payment infrastructure is pre-integrated for each country?</strong> Ask for a written list of filings the provider performs on your behalf and those you keep. Manual configuration per country adds compliance risk.</li><li><strong>Are your APIs aligned with TM Forum Open APIs?</strong> TM Forum publishes Open APIs for functions such as product catalog (TMF620) and product ordering (TMF622). Standards-aligned APIs reduce lock-in and ease integration; ask which APIs and versions are implemented and tested.</li><li><strong>Can I expose connectivity or bundles to my customers through your BSS?</strong> Bundling connectivity with third-party services is a differentiator for some brands. Confirm catalog, pricing and settlement support rather than assuming it.</li><li><strong>How do you support device-level security and regulation such as the EU Cyber Resilience Act?</strong> The Act covers products with digital elements; its reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026 and the main obligations from 11 December 2027. Manufacturers carry the legal duty, so ask what the platform provides to help you meet it, such as update and vulnerability workflows.</li><li><strong>Can billing support split payments and multi-party invoicing?</strong> Test your real payer structure in a trial tenant. See the billing guide for the payer, administrator and user model.</li><li><strong>How do you prevent and detect revenue leakage at scale?</strong> Ask what is reconciled, how often, who sees exceptions and how corrections are recorded.</li><li><strong>Can I bring my existing operator agreements onto your platform and manage them centrally?</strong> Bring Your Own Network support keeps commercial flexibility. Ask what integration work each additional operator requires and who pays for it.</li></ol>
<h2 id="network-ops">Network access, operations and security</h2>
<p>Beyond the platform, test what sits around it. These are the questions the OSS/BSS discussion often skips.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Network access.</strong> Which party contracts for carrier access, and does that arrangement permit your offer? Separate domestic coverage from roaming, and ask about the specific voice, messaging, data and device functions you need. A coverage map alone cannot answer these. Ask which operators the provider has direct agreements with, and for service-specific limits in writing.</li><li><strong>Support and escalation.</strong> What hours, severity definitions and escalation paths exist, including the route to the host network? Who talks to your customers while suppliers investigate?</li><li><strong>Resilience.</strong> What availability, recovery time and recovery point objectives are committed, and how are incidents communicated? Ask for the commitments in the contract, not a general statement.</li><li><strong>Analytics.</strong> Which dashboards exist (activation success, churn, usage), and can you pull raw data out for your own BI and finance tools?</li><li><strong>Failure handling.</strong> Provisioning failures, billing mismatches and network anomalies will happen. Check that the platform detects them, shows them to your team and supports recovery without engineering tickets.</li></ul>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Treat &#8220;launch in days&#8221; as a claim to test.</strong> Launch speed depends on your market, regulatory position, integrations and the network agreement. Ask for the dated steps that make up the timeline, and compare the provider&#8217;s statement with the <a href="/mvno/how-mvnes-accelerate-mvno-launch/">launch speed guide</a> and your own plan.</p></div>
<h2 id="scoring">Score the evidence, not the demo</h2>
<p>Once you have answers, score them in a way another evaluator could reproduce. The following scale is a proposed procurement method, not an industry benchmark.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Score</th><th>Meaning</th><th>What to record</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>0: unverified</td><td>Missing, unclear or unsupported answer</td><td>Open question, the artifact needed and an owner</td></tr><tr><td>1: documented</td><td>Written scope or reference exists, not yet accepted for your service</td><td>Document version, conditions and the remaining test</td></tr><tr><td>2: accepted</td><td>Your agreed test passed for the specified scope</td><td>Test record, environment, date and sign-off</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>For preferences, give each a weight from 1 to 3 by business priority, then multiply weight by evidence score and compare totals only across identical requirements. Keep mandatory gates in a separate pass or fail register. No universal percentage defines a safe shortlist, and a roadmap statement does not earn an accepted score.</p>
<p>Mark every capability as available in the proposed scope, separately supplied, custom work or roadmap. For anything unfinished, ask for the dependency and the acceptance condition. A demo built for another carrier or account model is background; it does not close a requirement for your deployment.</p>
<h2 id="acceptance">Acceptance tests that expose ownership</h2>
<p>The best tests show who owns a problem as well as whether a feature exists. Run one full customer transaction in a test environment, then introduce controlled exceptions. Trace the same subscription across the sales channel, the platform, the network status and billing, and record identifiers and timestamps. An accepted request may still be waiting for a downstream action, so the final service state matters.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Test scenario</th><th>Evidence to request</th><th>Question it answers</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Activation request times out and is retried</td><td>Request identifiers, final subscription state, charges</td><td>Did recovery create one intended service without duplicate work?</td></tr><tr><td>Usage events arrive late or out of order</td><td>Event log and reconciliation record</td><td>Which system decides the current state?</td></tr><tr><td>Plan changes near a billing boundary</td><td>Effective time, usage treatment, invoice explanation</td><td>Can support explain and correct the result?</td></tr><tr><td>A reseller user requests another account&#8217;s data</td><td>Authorized isolation test and access log</td><td>Are account boundaries enforced for that operation?</td></tr><tr><td>Data export imported elsewhere</td><td>Field definitions, identifier mapping, import result</td><td>Can your team use the records without the provider&#8217;s portal?</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Source: editorial acceptance scenarios. They are proposed tests, not claims that any named provider has passed them.</p>
<p>Two public references help you frame the questions. Stripe&#8217;s documentation on <a href="https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests" target="_blank" rel="noopener">idempotent requests</a> shows one way to retry an operation safely without performing it twice; ask how the MVNE&#8217;s own API handles duplicates and does not assume it matches another vendor. For account isolation, the OWASP API Security project describes <a href="https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0xa1-broken-object-level-authorization" target="_blank" rel="noopener">broken object-level authorization</a>, where a user who can reach an endpoint can also read objects that belong to someone else. Agree on test accounts and explicit authorization before any security testing.</p>

<h2 id="rfp">Build the RFP around the same structure</h2>
<p>An RFP is the checklist in procurement form. Structure it so every provider can be compared line by line.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>RFP section</th><th>What to include</th><th>Why it matters</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Service brief</td><td>Customers, countries, devices, services, channels, retained systems</td><td>Gives every bidder the same job</td></tr><tr><td>Mandatory gates</td><td>Pass or fail requirements with an acceptance test each</td><td>Stops preferences hiding a failed essential</td></tr><tr><td>Weighted preferences</td><td>Requirements, weights 1 to 3, evidence expected</td><td>Makes scoring reproducible</td></tr><tr><td>Commercial schedule</td><td>Setup, recurring, usage, support and exit charges on one template</td><td>Allows like-for-like pricing</td></tr><tr><td>Dependencies and exclusions</td><td>What the bidder needs from you and what is out of scope</td><td>Reveals hidden work</td></tr><tr><td>Acceptance plan</td><td>Test scenarios, environment, owners, sign-off rules</td><td>Turns promises into evidence</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Ask bidders to label each response as available now, separately supplied, custom work or roadmap, and to attach evidence references instead of sensitive subscriber data. Record your assumptions so the decision can be explained later.</p>
<h2 id="pricing">Pricing, service levels and data</h2>
<p>Compare pricing at the level of the same proposed service. A lower platform fee can leave more operating work with your team, so price what you keep as well as what you buy. Use the <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">MVNO launch cost guide</a> to organize assumptions, then replace estimates with written quotes. This article gives no price ranges because they vary by scope.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Commercial area</th><th>Clarify in the quote</th><th>Work you keep</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Setup and integration</td><td>Included environments, connectors and acceptance scope</td><td>Your implementation, testing and change management</td></tr><tr><td>Recurring and usage charges</td><td>Billing unit, minimums, tiers, adjustments</td><td>Reconciliation and forecast review</td></tr><tr><td>Support and incidents</td><td>Hours, severity definitions, escalation boundaries</td><td>Customer communications and internal operations</td></tr><tr><td>Exit and migration</td><td>Exports, assistance, notice periods, fees</td><td>Import validation, continuity and replacement operations</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>A service level agreement should name the service measured, exclusions, maintenance treatment, reporting method and escalation path. Separate platform availability from host-network service, and response targets from resolution commitments. Ask for remedies and dependencies, and review them with the people who will operate the service.</p>
<p>For data held by a provider, security and legal teams should review data locations, access controls and responsibilities for each market. NIST&#8217;s <a href="https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/144/final" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SP 800-144</a> gives foundational guidance on security and privacy for public cloud computing; it dates from 2011 and is not a current telecom compliance checklist.</p>
<h2 id="gaps">Common gaps and exit readiness</h2>
<p>Many platforms perform well in controlled conditions and struggle at real scale. Five gaps recur.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Speed without adaptability.</strong> A fast launch matters less than being able to change pricing, operators or markets without a re-implementation.</li><li><strong>Weak failure handling.</strong> Failures that are hidden or need engineering fixes become customer problems.</li><li><strong>No multi-entity support.</strong> Running several brands or legal entities then needs several platforms. See the <a href="/mvno/white-label-mvno/">white-label MVNO guide</a> for how branding and ownership are usually split.</li><li><strong>Locked data.</strong> You need usage, financial and operational data in documented formats, not only a portal view.</li><li><strong>Entangled logic.</strong> If provisioning rules, billing rules and data ownership sit in proprietary workflows, leaving becomes a rebuild.</li></ul>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Exit check.</strong> An export button proves a file can be produced. A successful sample import, documented identifiers and an agreed transition plan prove it is useful. Data export also does not by itself transfer network access or keep service running, so review continuity and network arrangements separately. Ask for a sample export before signing.</p></div>
<p>Exit readiness is not a sign you expect to churn. It is evidence of architectural maturity, because networks, markets and commercial models will change.</p>
<h2 id="case">What a published reference can and cannot tell you</h2>
<p>A reference case helps you form the right questions. RocketPhone.ai Limited, a London conversation-intelligence company for Salesforce and a certified Salesforce ISV listed on AppExchange, wanted a branded business mobile network that would capture native mobile calls without building telecom infrastructure. Its <a href="/spenza-product/rocketphone-case-study/">published case study</a> lists delivered capabilities including a white-label portal, API-driven SIM and eSIM provisioning, multi-tier reseller and enterprise account management, automated billing and subscription management, and SIP routing for enterprise voice.</p>
<p>Read that as delivered scope for one use case. It does not state a subscriber cohort, dated milestones or an acceptance score, so it cannot prove your timeline. For a comparable procurement, ask the provider to demonstrate the communications path, account hierarchy, operating permissions and support workflow your own product needs, and keep that evidence alongside your tests and contract review. If you want to walk through your own list, you can <a href="/contact-us/">talk to the Spenza team</a> about your operating model.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion: choose the platform you can verify</h2>
<p>In 2026, MVNOs will compete less on wholesale rates and more on how well their OSS/BSS lets them change networks, pricing and markets. Choose an MVNE when its architecture, automation, data ownership and integration fit the service in your brief, and when your own tests have shown it. Define gates first, score preferences by evidence, price the work you retain, and confirm the exit path before you commit. For the cost side of the decision, see the <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">launch cost guide</a>; for the US specifics, the <a href="/mvno/launch-mvno-us-guide/">US launch guide</a>.</p>
