<p>Single-carrier IoT is risky because every device you deploy depends on one mobile operator for network access, coverage, technology support and price. If that operator fails, retires a network or reprices, the whole fleet is affected at once and you have no ready alternative. A multi-network design, with more than one usable profile per device, rules for switching, and contracts that allow it, removes most of that exposure.</p>
<p>This guide sets out what counts as single-carrier IoT, the seven risks that matter in practice, how carrier lock-in turns those risks into costs, and what a resilient design needs. It uses one documented outage, FCC findings on the February 2024 AT&amp;T Mobility failure, and avoids unsourced loss figures. Where numbers depend on your fleet, we show you what to count instead of quoting a number.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-single-carrier-iot">What is single-carrier IoT?</h2>
<p>Single-carrier IoT is a deployment where connected devices rely on one mobile network operator (MNO) for cellular connectivity. In practice it means three things are bound to the same operator: the device identity (the IMSI on the SIM), the network access path (the APN and any firewall, NAT or routing built around it), and the commercial terms (price, roaming rights, term and exit).</p>
<p>The model is common because it is simple. One contract, one SIM type, one portal. The cost shows up later, when the fleet is large, the devices are hard to reach and the operator&rsquo;s priorities change. These patterns make a deployment fragile:</p>
<ul><li>One IMSI and one static profile per device, with no backup profile that can actually attach.</li><li>One APN path with central dependencies, such as firewall rules, NAT or static routes that assume a single operator.</li><li>Contracts that restrict portability, eUICC usage rights or any form of failover.</li><li>Alerts and automation tied to a single carrier&rsquo;s portal or data feeds.</li></ul>
<p>One clarification matters for design. &ldquo;Carrier&rdquo; here means the host network, not the brand on the SIM. A mobile virtual network operator (MVNO) SIM or a roaming SIM can still ride a single host network. If the host fails, so does the SIM. Real diversity means credentials that can attach to different physical networks. Our guide to <a href="/iot/what-is-m2m-machine-to-machine-sims/">M2M SIMs</a> covers how SIM types differ.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-matters-outage">What a real outage looks like</h2>
<p>The clearest public record is the FCC&rsquo;s report on the nationwide AT&amp;T Mobility outage of 22 February 2024. According to the <a href="https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-404150A1.pdf" rel="noopener">FCC Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau&rsquo;s findings</a>, all voice and 5G data services were unavailable for AT&amp;T Mobility users, affecting more than 125 million registered devices, blocking more than 92 million voice calls and preventing more than 25,000 calls to 911 call centers. The trigger was a network change with an equipment configuration error. Rolling it back took close to two hours, but full restoration took at least 12 hours because registration systems were overwhelmed when devices tried to re-register.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>The detail that matters for IoT:</strong> the same FCC report states that voice and 5G data were also unavailable to MVNO users and to other customers roaming on that network. A second SIM brand riding the same host network gave no protection. The failure was a configuration error, not a cable cut or an attack, which is why you cannot design around one &ldquo;likely&rdquo; cause.</p></div>
<p>The report does not break out IoT devices, but the mechanics apply to fleets: one change inside one operator can take every attached device offline, and recovery is slow even after the fix because millions of devices reconnect at once.</p>

<h2 id="risks-of-single-carrier-iot">The seven risks of single-carrier IoT</h2>
<h3>1. A single point of failure</h3>
<p>Outages come from fiber cuts, power loss, software faults, cyberattacks and misconfigurations like the one above. With one carrier, any of them can interrupt telemetry, remote commands and alerts for the entire fleet. You can negotiate a service level, but you cannot negotiate a second network into existence after the fact.</p>
<h3>2. Coverage gaps and congestion</h3>
<p>No operator covers everywhere equally. Basements, rural depots, ports, tunnels and industrial buildings leave blind spots, and the blind spots differ by operator. A device in a poor-signal spot on its one permitted network stays there, while another operator might serve the same address well. Congestion can also hit one network at a time. Real-time uses such as fleet tracking and remote monitoring feel this first. The choice of radio technology matters as well; see <a href="/esim/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap/">NB-IoT, LTE-M and 5G RedCap</a> for how coverage differs by technology.</p>
<h3>3. Network sunsets and technology lock-in</h3>
<p>Operators retire older networks to reuse spectrum. In the United States, the <a href="https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/plan-ahead-phase-out-3g-cellular-networks-and-service" rel="noopener">FCC&rsquo;s consumer guidance on the 3G phase-out</a> lists the major carriers&rsquo; 2022 shutdown schedules and notes that connected devices beyond phones, including medical devices, vehicle SOS services and home security systems, may rely on 3G. A device that supports only a retired network stops working whatever the contract says. Each operator also decides which low-power technologies it deploys and where, so your product roadmap quietly follows theirs.</p>
<h3>4. Policy shifts and roaming uncertainty</h3>
<p>Pricing, roaming agreements and throttling rules change. Cross-border fleets are especially exposed, because a SIM that works abroad through permanent roaming depends on commercial agreements the end customer does not control. In Europe, BEREC&rsquo;s <a href="https://www.berec.europa.eu/system/files/2025-03/BoR%20(25)%2048%20BEREC%20input%20to%20the%20EC%20on%20the%20Roaming%20Regulation.pdf" rel="noopener">March 2025 input to the European Commission</a> concluded that no further regulatory measures were needed on permanent roaming and M2M/IoT for now, while stressing that these markets should keep being monitored. In other words, the rules are not settled enough to build a ten-year plan on one operator&rsquo;s arrangement.</p>
<h3>5. Cost inflexibility</h3>
<p>With one carrier you lose pricing leverage once devices are in the field. Total cost of ownership drifts upward through SIM swaps, field visits, engineering time spent on a bespoke integration and rates you cannot compare. Count the site visits and engineering hours spent on carrier-specific work, using your own data. For how plan structures affect spend, see our <a href="/telecom/iot-connectivity-cost-guide-2025/">IoT connectivity cost guide</a>.</p>
<h3>6. Operational overhead and support bottlenecks</h3>
<p>When one carrier owns every diagnosis, you wait on its support queue and its tooling. If a field visit does not fix the problem, the device is replaced or reconfigured by hand. A second network gives you a way to test whether a fault is the device or the carrier, which shortens troubleshooting.</p>
<h3>7. A security and resilience monoculture</h3>
<p>Putting every device behind one network, one core and one set of credentials concentrates risk. A breach, a bad update or a configuration error then has a fleet-wide blast radius. Diversity across networks does not replace device security, but it limits how much one operator&rsquo;s failure can take down.</p>
<h2 id="carrier-lock-in">How carrier lock-in turns risk into cost</h2>
<p>Carrier lock-in is what makes the seven risks expensive. The device stays on one network because the SIM is fixed, the contract is closed or the modem supports one set of bands. Changing that later means visiting every site. Four industrial examples show how this plays out.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Deployment</th><th>Why lock-in bites</th><th>What reduces it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Smart metering</td><td>A 10 to 15 year device life outlasts several network generations. A meter that supports only a retiring technology can be stranded mid-life.</td><td>Hardware that supports the technologies you expect to need, plus remotely manageable profiles.</td></tr><tr><td>Remote tank monitoring</td><td>Remote sites with patchy coverage. A single-operator device goes dark where that operator is weak.</td><td>A second operator profile for sites where one network is weak, with failover rules.</td></tr><tr><td>Pumps, HVAC and rooftop equipment</td><td>Hard-to-reach installs. Any SIM change becomes a site visit, multiplied across the fleet.</td><td>Remote profile management so a carrier change does not need access to the unit.</td></tr><tr><td>Connected generators</td><td>Long service life while LTE-M and NB-IoT variants and bands evolve.</td><td>Profile agility and modules chosen for future bands, not only today&rsquo;s.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>A hypothetical shows the pattern. A utility installs meters expected to last 15 years on a physical SIM from one national carrier. Midway through the meters&rsquo; life that carrier announces it will retire the network technology the meters use. The utility must either replace hardware that still works or send crews to replace every SIM. The same utility with remotely manageable eUICC profiles, hardware that can use the replacement network and a contract that permits a new profile can handle the change from an operations console. Each of those three conditions is necessary; the eSIM alone is not enough.</p>

<h2 id="single-vs-multi-carrier">Single-carrier vs multi-carrier IoT</h2>
<p>The table compares the two models across the dimensions that decide results. It describes design properties, not guaranteed outcomes: a multi-carrier design only performs as designed if it is tested.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Dimension</th><th>Single-carrier</th><th>Multi-carrier</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Resilience</td><td>One network is a single point of failure.</td><td>Risk is spread, and policy-based failover can move traffic.</td></tr><tr><td>Coverage</td><td>Limited to one network&rsquo;s footprint.</td><td>Profiles can be chosen per region or device group.</td></tr><tr><td>Technology roadmap</td><td>Follows one carrier&rsquo;s timeline.</td><td>Adapts across several roadmaps.</td></tr><tr><td>Time to repair</td><td>One support path.</td><td>Parallel paths; you can test one network against another.</td></tr><tr><td>Lifecycle cost</td><td>Lower entry price; field visits and swaps appear later.</td><td>More governance up front; more control over spend.</td></tr><tr><td>Operations</td><td>More site visits and escalations for network faults.</td><td>More faults handled by remote profile changes.</td></tr><tr><td>Global scale</td><td>Constrained by roaming terms and local rules.</td><td>Local profiles per country where available.</td></tr><tr><td>Commercial leverage</td><td>Low negotiation power after deployment.</td><td>Switching is a credible option at renewal.</td></tr><tr><td>Tooling</td><td>Carrier-specific portals and feeds.</td><td>One management layer across networks.</td></tr><tr><td>Future-proofing</td><td>Tied to one carrier&rsquo;s retirements.</td><td>Technology can move with the best available network.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<h2 id="what-to-do-instead">What to do instead: a practical multi-carrier design</h2>
<h3>Design for carrier diversity from the start</h3>
<p>Choose modules and SIM formats that can hold or switch between more than one operator&rsquo;s credentials. Decide which events should trigger a switch: no registration, repeated attach failures, no data session or breach of a service threshold. Then check that your backend does not assume a single operator&rsquo;s addressing or firewall rules.</p>
<h3>Know the difference between eSIM, multi-IMSI and multi-carrier</h3>
<p>These terms are often mixed up:</p>
<ul><li><strong>eSIM or eUICC:</strong> a secure element that stores operator profiles and lets them be managed remotely.</li><li><strong>Multi-IMSI:</strong> more than one subscriber identity associated with a SIM or eSIM, which can support connectivity across networks depending on how the provider built it.</li><li><strong>Remote SIM provisioning (RSP):</strong> the standardized process for downloading, enabling, disabling and deleting profiles over the air.</li><li><strong>Multi-carrier connectivity:</strong> the outcome you want, using profiles, identities or network relationships to give a device more than one real path.</li></ul>
<p>For IoT devices without a screen or a user, GSMA&rsquo;s <a href="https://gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/SGP.32-v1.2.pdf" rel="noopener">SGP.32 specification</a> defines the architecture for managing eUICC profiles remotely, with an eSIM IoT remote manager (eIM) performing profile operations. It is the standardized route to profile management for these devices; our <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 vs SGP.32 guide</a> explains how it differs from the consumer standard. The standard defines the architecture. Whether a particular device can change carriers still depends on its modem, its profile provider and the participating operators.</p>
<h3>What eSIM cannot do</h3>
<p>Two limits are worth stating plainly. First, eSIM cannot make hardware support a radio technology it lacks. If the modem or firmware cannot operate on a future network, changing the profile does not help. Second, eSIM does not by itself give automatic failover. Reliable failover needs compatible hardware, more than one usable profile, network availability where the device sits, switching rules and a platform that executes and monitors those rules. Evaluate the whole architecture, not the SIM in isolation.</p>
<h3>Set failover rules with guardrails</h3>
<p>Keep at least two usable profiles for devices where downtime has a cost. Set thresholds that avoid flapping, where a device bounces between networks. Use hold-down timers, preferred carriers by region, and limits on how often a device may switch. Test by cutting the primary network on purpose in a pilot, and record how long recovery actually takes. Plan the first-power case too: a device needs bootstrap connectivity to download its first working profile.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/multi-carrier-iot-failover-profiles-orange.webp" alt="One truck-mounted tracker linked by two cream ribbons to two different cell towers on separate hills, with one tower dimmed and the ribbon to the second tower glowing." width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A device with two usable profiles can move to a second physical network when the first one fails.</figcaption></figure>
<h3>Negotiate contracts that allow switching</h3>
<p>Technical flexibility is wasted if the contract blocks it. Ask for terms covering:</p>
<ul><li>Freedom to change or add profiles on devices you own, with no penalty.</li><li>Support for more than one operator on a device.</li><li>Service levels tied to measurable performance such as connection success or uptime, not only to network availability claims.</li><li>Remedies for major outages, and clear exit and data-return terms.</li><li>Reasonable notice before an operator retires a network technology.</li></ul>
<h2 id="impact-by-industry">Where single-carrier IoT fails first, by industry</h2>
<h3>Logistics: fleets, cold chain and asset tracking</h3>
<p>Lost telemetry means wrong ETAs, missed temperature alerts and gaps in depots and ports. Practices that help: a preferred carrier per route or region, a backup profile for weak-signal zones, and on-device buffering so data is stored during a gap and sent when the link returns. Cost patterns are covered in our guide to <a href="/iot/fleet-automotive-iot-connectivity-costs/">fleet and automotive connectivity costs</a>.</p>
<h3>Utilities: metering and distributed assets</h3>
<p>Missed meter reads and failed remote commands generate site visits. Distribute profiles by grid segment where coverage differs, cache data locally, and set emergency switching rules for weather events. Long device lives make network sunsets the main planning risk.</p>
<h3>Healthcare: remote patient monitoring</h3>
<p>A missed alert can matter clinically. Use stricter switching thresholds, test failover as part of release checks, and keep an alert path that does not depend on the same network. See <a href="/telecom/iot-connectivity-healthcare-remote-patient-monitoring/">IoT connectivity for remote patient monitoring</a> for requirements specific to care settings.</p>
<h2 id="migration">Moving from one carrier to many: a roadmap</h2>
<ol><li><strong>Audit what you have.</strong> List devices by modem, SIM format (removable or soldered), supported technologies, countries, contract end date and exit terms. This shows which devices can move now, which wait for hardware replacement and where the contract is the blocker.</li><li><strong>Quantify the exposure.</strong> Count the devices per carrier and region, the cost of an hour offline per use case, the number of site visits per year caused by connectivity, and the renewal date. These are your inputs; no outside average replaces them.</li><li><strong>Pick a management layer.</strong> You need one place to see profiles, usage and events across networks, apply switching rules and reconcile spend. Our guide to <a href="/telecom/single-pane-of-glass-iot-connectivity-management/">single-pane IoT connectivity management</a> describes what to look for, and <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">this comparison of connectivity management platforms</a> lists the criteria.</li><li><strong>Run a pilot cohort.</strong> Add a second profile to a subset. Track per-carrier connection success, test failover on purpose in a maintenance window, and measure recovery time.</li><li><strong>Scale by region or device type</strong> and use each renewal to secure portability, eUICC rights and performance-based terms.</li></ol>
<h2 id="deployment-checklist">Pre-deployment checklist</h2>
<ul><li><strong>eUICC compatibility:</strong> does the module support the eSIM architecture and form factor you plan to use?</li><li><strong>Radio compatibility:</strong> LTE, LTE-M, NB-IoT, 5G and band support for every target market.</li><li><strong>Profile availability:</strong> are operator profiles available in each country where devices will run?</li><li><strong>RSP architecture:</strong> which GSMA specification do the device and the provider support?</li><li><strong>Failover policy:</strong> when and why may a device change profile?</li><li><strong>Bootstrap connectivity:</strong> how does a new device get its first connection?</li><li><strong>Observability:</strong> can the platform show profile status, usage, connectivity events and failures?</li><li><strong>Lifecycle:</strong> how are devices provisioned, migrated, suspended, retired and recovered remotely?</li></ul>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion: treat connectivity as a design choice</h2>
<p>Single-carrier IoT trades a simpler start for risks that grow with fleet size and device life: a network fault that reaches every device, coverage you cannot patch, technology retirements you do not control and a contract that weakens your position over time. The 2024 AT&amp;T Mobility outage, in which even MVNO and roaming users lost service, shows that a failure inside one host network is not hypothetical.</p>
<p>The remedy is a design, not a product: hardware that can use more than one network, remotely manageable profiles, switching rules you have tested, a management layer that spans operators, and contracts that let you act on all of it. Start with an audit of your devices and your contract, pilot failover on a small cohort and expand from the results. For wider context, see our <a href="/iot/iot-connectivity-comparison/">IoT connectivity comparison</a> and <a href="/iot/global-iot-connectivity-2026/">global IoT connectivity guide</a>.</p>
