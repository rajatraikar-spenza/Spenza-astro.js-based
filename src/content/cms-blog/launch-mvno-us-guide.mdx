<p><em>Regulatory facts last verified 26 October 2026.</em></p>
<p>A mobile virtual network operator (MVNO) sells mobile service under its own brand on another operator&#8217;s radio network. In the US you do not need spectrum or a wireless license to start one. You get network access on AT&amp;T, T-Mobile or Verizon, directly or through a partner, register with the FCC, set up SIMs, billing and support, and file recurring reports.</p>
<p>Most companies that ask this already have customers. Think of a device maker that wants to sell a plan with its hardware, an MSP or internet provider adding mobile lines, or a software platform that wants connectivity inside its product. For them the fastest US path is usually a light MVNO on an MVNE. This guide covers the three models, who files what with the FCC under each one, the timeline and what Spenza&#8217;s US launches looked like. It is a planning map, not legal advice.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-start-an-mvno-in-the-us">How do you start an MVNO in the US?</h2>
<p>To launch an MVNO in the US, define your customers and service, choose an operating model, secure wholesale network access and assign regulatory responsibilities. Then connect provisioning, billing and support, check the economics and run a controlled pilot before opening the service to more customers. In order:</p>
<ol><li>Decide what you will sell, to whom and on which devices.</li><li>Choose an operating model: branded reseller, light MVNO or full MVNO.</li><li>Get access to AT&amp;T, T-Mobile or Verizon, directly or through an MVNE or aggregator.</li><li>Map your FCC and state obligations, starting with one question: will you sell voice?</li><li>Set up SIMs and eSIMs, number porting, billing, taxes and a self-service portal.</li><li>Certify the devices you sell, if you sell hardware.</li><li>Price plans after taxes and wholesale costs, then pilot on real devices and launch.</li></ol>
<h2 id="the-opportunity-if-you-already-have-customers">The opportunity if you already have customers</h2>
<p>The hard part of a new mobile brand is acquiring subscribers. A company with an existing audience has already paid that cost: it has the distribution, the billing relationship and the support team. Adding mobile turns a one-time sale or a service contract into recurring connectivity revenue, and it keeps the customer inside your product instead of sending them to a carrier store. An existing audience gives you a distribution route, but demand and willingness to pay still need testing with real buyers.</p>

<h2 id="choose-your-mvno-model-what-you-own-and-what-you-rent">Choose your MVNO model: what you own and what you rent</h2>
<p>The three models differ in how much of the stack you run. An MVNE (mobile virtual network enabler) is the platform layer in the middle: it supplies provisioning, billing and the carrier integrations so you do not build them. Our guide to <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">MNO, MVNO, MVNE and MVNA roles</a> defines each party.</p>

<p>The cost gap between the models is roughly an order of magnitude at each step. The <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">MVNO launch cost breakdown</a> itemizes each tier, and the <a href="/mvno-calculator/">MVNO launch cost calculator</a> lets you model your own numbers. The OSS and BSS layer the MVNE supplies is explained in <a href="/telecom/oss-bss/">OSS and BSS in telecom</a>.</p>
<h2 id="get-network-access-at-t-t-mobile-or-verizon">Get network access: AT&amp;T, T-Mobile or Verizon</h2>
<p>The US has three national mobile networks: AT&amp;T, T-Mobile and Verizon. There are three ways to reach them:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Direct wholesale agreement.</strong> You negotiate with the carrier. Expect a business plan, volume forecasts, minimum commitments or deposits, and a long technical integration. In our experience the negotiation alone takes 6 to 12 months.</li><li><strong>Through an aggregator (MVNA).</strong> An aggregator holds the carrier contract and resells capacity to smaller MVNOs.</li><li><strong>Through an MVNE.</strong> The enabler already holds carrier agreements and integrations, so you sign one contract and configure plans on its platform.</li></ul>
<p>Before you sign, ask which services the access includes (voice, texting, data, 5G), which devices are eligible, what the wholesale rate card and minimums are, who handles escalations and what happens to your lines if you leave. Access to two networks protects coverage and gives you a fallback, which is why IMZ launched on both T-Mobile and Verizon.</p>
<p>If you sell hardware, plan for device certification early. Cellular devices need FCC equipment authorization, most carriers expect industry certification such as PTCRB or GCF, and some networks, Verizon among them, require their own certification as well. For device makers this is often the slowest item on the plan.</p>
<h2 id="us-compliance-who-files-what">US compliance: who files what</h2>
<p>There is no single &#8220;MVNO license&#8221; in the US. There is a set of registrations and recurring filings. Two questions decide which apply to you: do you sell voice, and who is the carrier of record? The carrier of record is the company legally responsible for the service.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/us-mvno-compliance-map-orange.webp" alt="A contract with a US map connects voice, data and regulatory filings" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"></figure>
<h3 id="voice-or-data-only">Voice or data-only?</h3>
<p>Plans with calling bring most of the federal telecom rules into play. Data-only plans, common for IoT devices and tablets, carry a much shorter list, and the federal Internet Tax Freedom Act shields internet access from state and local taxes. Adding voice to a bundle brings back the full regulatory list, while the internet access part can stay tax-exempt if you can separate it in your books. Have counsel confirm how each of your plans is classified.</p>
<h3 id="the-obligations">The obligations</h3>

<p>Two more points come up in every launch review. There is no federal SIM registration law, but CPNI rules require you to authenticate customers before discussing their accounts, and the robocall rules expect voice providers to know their customers. The FCC also adopted SIM swap and port-out fraud protections in November 2023. Their compliance date was tied to federal paperwork review, so check the current status, and build strong authentication into SIM changes and port-outs from day one.</p>

<h2 id="price-for-margin-after-tax">Price for margin after tax</h2>
<p>Work out contribution per line before you publish a price. Start with the plan price excluding pass-through taxes, then subtract the wholesale network cost, platform fees, payment fees, support and any channel commission. Divide your acquisition cost by that monthly contribution to get payback in months. Then decide how you show taxes. At the national average, a $25 plan reaches the customer at about $32 after taxes and fees, and that gap drives churn when it comes as a surprise.</p>
<p>Model heavy users as well as typical ones, and check the cash timing: deposits and minimum commitments can leave you paying the network before customers pay you. Unbilled lines and carrier invoice errors are where margins leak, so read our list of <a href="/mvno/mvno-telecom-reseller-revenue-leakage/">telecom revenue leaks in MVNO billing</a> before launch.</p>

<h2 id="five-mistakes-that-delay-us-launches">Five mistakes that delay US launches</h2>
<ol><li>Signing for network access before deciding whether you will sell voice, which changes the whole compliance list.</li><li>Pricing before taxes and fees, then losing customers when the first bill arrives about 29 percent higher.</li><li>Treating eSIM as optional when US models of the iPhone 14 and later have no SIM tray.</li><li>Leaving porting and port-out authentication until the end, when they are the main conversion and fraud risks.</li><li>Having no named owner for the recurring dates: March 1 for CPNI and robocall recertification, April 1 for Form 499-A.</li></ol>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Starting an MVNO in the US takes no spectrum and, with an MVNE, no telecom build. It does take three decisions made early: which model you will run, whether you will sell voice and who will be the carrier of record. Get those right and the rest is configuration, testing and a filing calendar with named owners.</p>
<p>If you already have customers, the economics favor you. You have the distribution and the billing relationship that new mobile brands spend most of their budget acquiring. The work is choosing the lightest model that still gives you control of pricing and the customer.</p>
<h2 id="faqs">FAQs</h2>
<div class="mh-faq">
<details name="mh-faq" open><summary><h3 id="faq-1">Do you need an FCC license to start an MVNO?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>No. An MVNO uses a host operator&#8217;s licensed spectrum, so it needs no spectrum license of its own. It still has to register with the FCC and file annual reports. If it sells voice, customer-data, 911 and robocall rules apply too, and international calling needs international Section 214 authority. Under a branded reseller arrangement in which the carrier of record contracts with and bills your customers, the carrier of record carries those duties.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-2">How much does it cost to start an MVNO in the US?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Spenza&#8217;s published estimates are $10K to $50K for a branded reseller, $100K to $400K in the first year for a light MVNO on an MVNE, and $2M to $10M or more for a full MVNO with its own core. The <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">MVNO launch cost breakdown</a> itemizes each range.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-3">How long does it take to launch an MVNO?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>A branded reseller or light MVNO on an MVNE can go live in weeks to a few months, depending on devices, voice support and testing. A full MVNO that negotiates directly with a carrier and builds its own core and systems usually needs 12 to 18 months.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-4">Do you need a direct contract with AT&amp;T, T-Mobile or Verizon?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>No. Most new MVNOs reach a national network through an MVNE or an aggregator that already holds the carrier agreement. A direct wholesale contract makes sense when you can commit large volumes and staff the integration, testing and compliance work it brings.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-5">What is a carrier of record?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>The carrier of record is the company legally responsible for the telecom service a customer receives: it registers with the FCC, files the required reports and answers for compliance. A branded reseller usually sells under another company&#8217;s carrier of record. A light or full MVNO is normally its own carrier of record.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-6">Do MVNOs have to file in the Robocall Mitigation Database?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes, if they resell voice. FCC Enforcement Advisory 2026-02 states that MVNOs reselling voice are voice service providers. They must file a certification and a robocall mitigation plan that describes their know-your-customer process, and recertify every year by March 1.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-7">Can you launch an eSIM-only MVNO in the US?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes. US models of the iPhone 14 and later are eSIM-only, so eSIM support is required anyway. Going eSIM-only removes SIM logistics, but older phones and many IoT devices still need physical SIMs. See how <a href="/esim/esim-only-mvnos-2025/">eSIM-only MVNOs</a> handle activation and support.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-8">Can an MSP or internet provider add mobile service?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes. MSPs and internet providers already own the billing relationship, support team and local brand that an MVNO needs. Most start as a branded reseller or light MVNO, bundle mobile lines with existing services and bill everything on one invoice. Our guide to <a href="/mvno/isp-to-mvno-convergence/">adding mobile as an ISP</a> covers the bundle.</p></div></details>
