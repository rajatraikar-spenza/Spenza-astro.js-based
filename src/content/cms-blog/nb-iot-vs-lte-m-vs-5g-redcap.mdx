<p><strong>NB-IoT vs LTE-M comes down to how the device behaves. NB-IoT suits stationary devices that send small, infrequent reports and need deep indoor reach. LTE-M suits devices that move, send larger payloads, need faster response or must take firmware updates over the air. 5G RedCap is a third option for applications that need 5G New Radio at lower device complexity than full 5G.</strong> Neither low-power radio is automatically the longer-lasting one: battery life depends on the whole reporting cycle and on timers the network sets, not on the technology name.</p>
<p>The rest of this guide gives the head-to-head table, then the questions that change the answer: mobility, latency, battery, coverage, cost and what eSIM can and cannot do. It ends with an acceptance test you can run on real hardware. For the wider field of options, including LoRaWAN and satellite, see the <a href="/iot/iot-connectivity-comparison/">IoT connectivity comparison</a>. Treat every figure below as a starting hypothesis: a standard describes what a radio can do, and only a named module on a named network proves what your device will do.</p>

<h2 id="head-to-head">Head-to-head: NB-IoT vs LTE-M vs 5G RedCap</h2>
<p>NB-IoT and LTE-M are low-power wide-area cellular technologies that 3GPP standardized in Release 13. Reduced Capability (RedCap) is a 5G New Radio device class defined in Release 17. They overlap, but they target different workloads, and you should compare the named device category (for example Cat-NB1, Cat-M1) rather than the marketing label.</p>

<h3 id="shared">What NB-IoT and LTE-M have in common</h3>
<p>They are better understood as complementary than as rivals. Both are 3GPP standards, both run on licensed cellular spectrum, both support PSM and eDRX, both use cellular-grade authentication with a SIM or eSIM, and both were designed for large numbers of devices per cell. Many fleets use NB-IoT for fixed sensors and LTE-M for moving or higher-data devices, and run both under one management layer. A useful shorthand: NB-IoT is a heart monitor sending a pulse once an hour, while LTE-M is a smartwatch that syncs and moves with you.</p>

<h2 id="data-rate-mobility-latency">How do data rate, mobility and latency affect the choice?</h2>
<p>They matter only against the application&#8217;s delivery requirement. A moving tracker may need an unbroken session, while a meter can reconnect between reports. Measure the time from a device event to a usable record in your backend, including wake-up, network access, retries and processing.</p>
<p>For a quick NB-IoT vs LTE-M decision, shortlist NB-IoT for small, infrequent reports where delayed delivery is acceptable, and LTE-M when movement during an active session, larger transfers or frequent interaction matter. These are hypotheses to test, not guarantees.</p>
<h3 id="mobility">Separate movement from session continuity</h3>
<p>A device can travel between reporting locations without holding a session the whole way. That is different from handing an active connection from cell to cell. Cat-NB1 had no connected-mode mobility, Release 14 Cat-NB2 added some connection transfer between base stations, and LTE-M handles handover natively (Nordic Semiconductor Academy). Adoption of the NB-IoT mobility enhancements varies by operator, so do not assume them. Test your actual route and the recovery behavior after a cell change, and avoid copying a vehicle-speed threshold from a generic comparison.</p>
<h3 id="latency">Test deadlines, not advertised peaks</h3>
<p>Latency is where the two differ most in practice, and it depends heavily on coverage conditions. Ericsson&#8217;s NB-IoT overview puts uplink latency from about 1.6 seconds in good coverage to about 7.6 seconds at the deepest coverage extension (<a href="https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/ericsson-technology-review/articles/nb-iot-a-sustainable-technology-for-connecting-billions-of-devices" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ericsson Technology Review</a>). In Nordic&#8217;s field test on one Norwegian network, an LTE-M device connected from a cold start in roughly 3.4 to 4.8 seconds, against 17 to 28 seconds for NB-IoT. Those are single-network, single-hardware results, so use them as direction, not as a spec for your fleet.</p>
<p>Record message size, acknowledgement policy and acceptable delivery time. Include command delivery to a sleeping device. Peak radio rates leave out protocol overhead, signal quality and scheduling. RedCap deserves evaluation when its NR capabilities fit the workload, not because the roadmap says 5G.</p>
<h2 id="battery-life">How should NB-IoT vs LTE-M battery life be compared?</h2>
<p>Both technologies are designed for multi-year battery operation, and Ericsson cites a design target of more than 10 years with sustained reachability for NB-IoT using PSM and eDRX. That is a design target under idealized duty cycles, not a promise for your device. The honest answer to which lasts longer is that it depends on payload, coverage, retries, sleep current and network timers.</p>
<p>Nordic&#8217;s published field experiment shows why a fixed ranking fails. Over most of the distances tested, LTE-M used less charge per connection because it attached faster. NB-IoT used less in a few obstructed positions and reached farther. Nordic also found the NB-IoT cell used a longer RRC inactivity timer (18 s vs 11 s) and a longer connected-mode DRX cycle (2048 ms vs 320 ms), and when it simulated each network&#8217;s settings the average current came out at about 31 µA for LTE-M and 18 µA for NB-IoT. The author attributes that gap to the network configuration, not to the technology. Read the <a href="https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/nordic/nordic-blog/b/blog/posts/ltem-vs-nbiot-field-test-how-distance-affects-power-consumption" target="_blank" rel="noopener">full write-up</a>: it dates from 2022, used one nRF9160 module and static positions, and the network operator, not the device, set the timers.</p>
<p>Power Saving Mode (PSM) lets a device sleep and stay unreachable between reports. Extended DRX (eDRX) lengthens paging intervals. PSM arrived in Release 12 and eDRX in Release 13 (Ericsson). The settings a device requests and those the network grants can differ, so log both.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap-energy-cycle-orange.webp" alt="A tracker labelled Send, a Wi-Fi-style puck labelled Wait and a moon pillow labelled Sleep joined in a loop by a cream ribbon under a tower, showing the connect, send, wait and sleep cycle." width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Energy is spent across connection, transfer, waiting and sleep, so measure the whole loop.</figcaption></figure>
<h3 id="energy-budget">Build an energy budget you can reproduce</h3>
<p>Measure charge across connection establishment, transfer, waiting and sleep. Add failed attempts, recovery and firmware maintenance. Include the sensor, processor and power supply, because modem consumption alone is not device consumption. State usable battery capacity, operating temperature and service-life assumptions before you estimate a replacement interval.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Watch out:</strong> a device that sends less data can still use more energy if it searches repeatedly or waits on unsuitable timers. Use a successful report, with failed attempts included, as the comparison unit.</p></div>
<p>For a connected sensor deployment, align battery measurements with the real sampling and reporting schedule, and test long coverage loss explicitly, because retries can dominate an otherwise efficient cycle. The <a href="/iot/iot-sensors-types-connectivity/">IoT sensors and connectivity guide</a> covers how sensor type shapes that schedule.</p>
<h2 id="coverage">What determines coverage, roaming and global availability?</h2>
<p>Availability depends on the radio service, supported bands, device approval and subscription agreement together. An operator&#8217;s LTE or 5G footprint does not prove NB-IoT, LTE-M or RedCap is offered to your device. NB-IoT was designed with a 164 dB link-budget target, against about 144 dB for GPRS and 142.7 dB for LTE, which Ericsson equates to roughly 20 dB of added reach for deep indoor and underground sites. Treat that as a design target: actual indoor performance depends on the installation, antenna and local network.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/internet-of-things/deployment-map/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">GSMA Mobile IoT deployment map</a> shows which operators have launched LTE-M, NB-IoT or both, which helps build a shortlist. It does not promise indoor reception, roaming access or every optional feature. Ask the provider which serving networks and restrictions apply to your contracted scope; long-term roaming policies in particular vary by operator.</p>
<p>RedCap needs its own commercial check. T-Mobile&#8217;s <a href="https://www.t-mobile.com/news/network/5g-redcap-powering-smart-devices-with-smarter-5g" target="_blank" rel="noopener">RedCap update</a> describes one operator&#8217;s service and devices. It is evidence that RedCap is deployed in a specific context, not that every 5G subscription or module supports it.</p>
<h3 id="supported-combo">Write down the supported combination</h3>
<p>Record the module model, firmware, antenna design, bands, operator, subscription and enabled features, and keep that matrix versioned alongside the bill of materials. A firmware change or service migration can invalidate an earlier test. Pilot in weak-signal places, inside real enclosures and along real routes, and record failures as carefully as successes. One successful attach in one country is not evidence for a launch list. The same logic applies to single-network deployments: see the risks in the guide to <a href="/telecom/single-carrier-iot-risks-2025/">single-carrier IoT</a>.</p>
<h2 id="cost">How do module cost and total cost of ownership compare?</h2>
<p>Module price is one line in cellular IoT ownership cost. Certification, integration, connectivity charges, battery replacement, field visits and eventual migration can outweigh a small difference in module price. NB-IoT modules have generally been positioned as the cheaper option, but compare quotes for the same countries, volumes, traffic and support scope instead of relying on price bands. The <a href="/telecom/iot-connectivity-cost-guide-2025/">IoT connectivity cost guide</a> develops the commercial model separately.</p>

<p>Ask what happens to billing when devices are suspended, replaced or stored before activation, get separate quotes for configurations that pass technical screening, and record exclusions and support ownership. The cheapest data allowance is rarely the lowest lifecycle cost.</p>
<h2 id="redcap-esim">What changes with eRedCap, network evolution and eSIM?</h2>
<p>Enhanced RedCap (eRedCap) belongs to Release 18. It defines a reduced peak rate of 10 Mbps and an optional 5 MHz baseband bandwidth in FR1, with the radio-frequency bandwidth still 20 MHz. It is a lower-cost step down from RedCap, not an LTE-M replacement, so confirm which category a proposed module supports.</p>
<p>Release 17 also added satellite (non-terrestrial network) support for NB-IoT and LTE-M. That does not make existing devices satellite-ready: it takes a compatible module, firmware, bands and a commercial satellite service.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Definition:</strong> eSIM changes subscription profiles on compatible hardware. It does not add radio bands, an antenna or a modem technology, so it cannot turn an LTE-M modem into a RedCap one.</p></div>
<p>Provisioning is a separate layer from the radio; the <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 vs SGP.32 guide</a> and the <a href="/esim/iot-sim-cards-guide/">IoT SIM cards guide</a> explain it. An eSIM migration still needs compatible hardware, supported profiles and a recovery path, and remote provisioning will not remove every truck roll. On longevity, obtain each operator&#8217;s current support position and contractual notice terms instead of assuming a universal sunset year for NB-IoT or LTE-M.</p>
<h2 id="validate">How should you choose and validate a cellular IoT radio?</h2>
<p>Run a documented acceptance test on the supported device and service combination before fleet rollout. Define delivery, power, mobility and recovery requirements first, then keep radio logs and application records together so a successful attach cannot hide failed business data.</p>

<p>Define the reporting and command workload, including maintenance. Confirm the approved module, firmware, bands and service. Run comparable tests in normal and adverse conditions, reconcile device logs with received application records, and approve rollout only after assigning ownership for failure and recovery.</p>
<p>Spenza, an MVNE platform for connected-product operations, describes multi-country sensor rollout in its published <a href="/spenza-product/butlr-iot-spenza-casestudy/">Butlr case study</a>. That is evidence about coordinating activation across countries, not a ranking of NB-IoT, LTE-M or RedCap, so measure radio performance on your own devices. If a fleet mixes radio types and carriers, a single management layer keeps provisioning and monitoring consistent; see the <a href="/esim/esim-orchestration-global-iot/">eSIM orchestration guide</a> and the overview of <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">IoT connectivity management platforms</a>. Moving assets raise their own questions, covered in the <a href="/iot/iot-asset-tracking-connectivity-guide/">asset tracking connectivity guide</a>.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion: pick from behavior, prove it in the field</h2>
<p>Choose NB-IoT, LTE-M or RedCap from the application requirement and the commercial combination you can actually buy. Use standards to understand capability, operator evidence to confirm availability, and field measurements to approve the product. Keep power, delivery and recovery records comparable across candidates.</p>
<p>A radio that looks right on a specification sheet can still fail the installation or the operating model. The next step is a bounded pilot with named owners, documented conditions and thresholds agreed before results arrive, with an alternative configuration held in reserve until the data justifies the rollout.</p>
<h2 id="faqs">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<div class="mh-faq">
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>What is the main difference between NB-IoT and LTE-M?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>NB-IoT is built for small, infrequent reports from mostly stationary devices and offers deep coverage. LTE-M offers higher data rates, native handover and faster response, which suits moving devices and over-the-air updates. Both are 3GPP Release 13 technologies on licensed spectrum.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Which is better for battery-powered devices?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Neither always wins. NB-IoT is designed for very long life at low duty cycles, but a field test by Nordic Semiconductor found LTE-M used less energy in normal conditions because it connected faster, and that network timers drove much of the difference. Measure your own reporting cycle on the target network.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Can LTE-M be used for asset tracking?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes. LTE-M supports handover between cells and is the usual starting point for moving assets. Test your real routes, because coverage and recovery after losing signal vary by operator.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Can NB-IoT handle firmware updates?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>It can carry downlink data, so it is not a one-way technology. Practical updating depends on image size, throughput, energy and recovery from interruption. LTE-M is generally the easier fit for large images. Test a full update under weak coverage, including verification and rollback.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Can these radios support voice calls?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>NB-IoT is not a voice technology. LTE-M can support VoLTE where the operator offers it, and RedCap voice depends on the operator and device. Require a supported voice configuration and test calls on the intended network.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Do NB-IoT and LTE-M need a SIM or eSIM?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes. Both use cellular authentication, so a device needs a SIM or eSIM that the target network accepts. An eSIM changes subscription profiles but does not change the radio capability of the modem.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Can one module serve every launch country?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Sometimes, but it needs evidence. Supported bands, certification, operator approvals and subscription access must all line up. Keep a country-by-country matrix for the exact hardware and firmware, and plan a separate variant where a configuration is unsupported.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Can NB-IoT and LTE-M run in the same deployment?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Yes. Many fleets use NB-IoT for fixed sensors and LTE-M for mobile or higher-data devices. Managing both under one platform keeps provisioning, monitoring and billing consistent.</p></div></details>
