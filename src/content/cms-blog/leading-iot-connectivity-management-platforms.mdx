<h2 id="what-is-an-iot-cmp">What is an IoT connectivity management platform?</h2>
<p>An IoT connectivity management platform (CMP) is software that activates, monitors, controls and bills cellular and eSIM connections across many devices and carriers. It manages SIM lifecycle states, data usage, rate plans, alerts and APIs, so an enterprise can run thousands of devices from one console instead of separate carrier portals.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/iot-cmp-architecture-orange.webp" alt="Devices connect through a central platform console to multiple carrier towers and a cloud app" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The CMP sits between your devices, several carriers and your business systems.</figcaption></figure>

<h2 id="capabilities">What a CMP must do: 8 capabilities to test</h2>
<p>A good IoT CMP covers eight jobs: SIM and eSIM lifecycle, multi-carrier access, bulk operations, usage monitoring, rate plans and billing, APIs, security, and automation. Platforms differ in how much of each they support, and some actions depend on the carrier and the device, so test each one on your own devices during a trial. A demo account with ten SIMs hides how the platform behaves at 50,000.</p>
<h3 id="1-sim-and-esim-lifecycle-sgp02-sgp22-sgp32">1. SIM and eSIM lifecycle (SGP.02, SGP.22, SGP.32)</h3>
<p>Every SIM moves through states: test ready, inventory, activated, suspended and deactivated. The CMP should move SIMs between states automatically (test ready to activated on first data session), so you stop paying for SIMs in a warehouse.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/iot-sim-lifecycle-orange.webp" alt="A SIM moves from inventory through testing and activation to suspension and a locked deactivated state" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>eSIM adds a second layer: which operator profile is active on the chip. <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Three GSMA specifications</a> matter:</p>

<p>GSMA published SGP.32 version 1.0 in May 2023; the current version, <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/SGP.32-v1.3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1.3</a>, is dated 22 May 2026. Ask every vendor which version runs in production, which eIM it uses, and which eUICC makers it has certified.</p>
<h3 id="2-multi-carrier-multi-imsi-and-esim-profile-switching">2. Multi-carrier, multi-IMSI and eSIM profile switching</h3>
<p>A multi-IMSI SIM holds several network identities and an applet on the SIM picks one. An eUICC downloads complete operator profiles. Multi-IMSI is simpler and cheaper. eUICC lets you load a local profile where a country restricts permanent roaming, if the device, the eUICC and the operator all support the switch. Our <a href="/esim/multi-imsi-vs-euicc-guide-iot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">multi-IMSI vs eUICC guide</a> compares the two in detail.</p>
<h3 id="3-bulk-operations-how-does-a-cmp-update-thousands-of-devices-at-once">3. Bulk operations: how does a CMP update thousands of devices at once?</h3>
<p>A CMP that supports bulk operations applies one change to a device group: you select the group by tag or attribute, set the new plan, policy or eSIM profile, test it on a small batch, then roll out in waves. The better platforms report a result for every device.</p>
<p>Recovery is less certain. A device that loses connectivity after a profile switch may be unreachable for a switch back, so prove the fallback before a fleet-wide rollout.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/iot-bulk-flow-orange.webp" alt="One update console sends a change to many devices; most confirm and two failed devices loop back for retry" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>Questions to ask before you sign:</p>
<ul>
<li>What is the largest single job the platform runs, and how many devices per hour does it process?</li>
<li>Can you run the same job through the API and get a per-device result back?</li>
<li>Does it support a dry run and scheduled waves, for example 1 percent, then 10 percent, then the rest?</li>
<li>What happens to a device that is offline when the job runs, or that drops off the network after the change?</li>
</ul>
<p>On Spenza, the Partner API changes one SIM per call and accepts an Idempotency-Key header on writes, so a retried job replays instead of repeating a change. SIM groups carry their own spend limits and data quotas. The <a href="https://docs.spenza.com/rate-limits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">published rate limit</a> is 120 requests a minute per account by default, so run large jobs in waves.</p>
<p><strong>A real workflow: moving a SIM group to a new plan.</strong> Spenza runs this on its own account through its <a href="/spenza-product/spenza-mcp-server-ai/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MCP server</a>, and the same calls work directly against the API:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li>Read the group&#8217;s membership (for example, every SIM in Field Sales).</li>
<li>Resolve the exact plan name from the plan catalog.</li>
<li>Schedule the plan purchase for each SIM, effective the first of next month.</li>
<li>Poll each transaction&#8217;s status to confirm the purchase is scheduled.</li>
</ol>
<p>A timeout mid-job is safe to retry, because the idempotency key stops the plan from being scheduled twice. Failed activations surface the same way: when Spenza provisions an eSIM and the carrier rejects the IMEI, the status call returns the reason instead of an activation QR that will never work.</p>

<h3 id="5-rate-plans-pooling-and-billing">5. Rate plans, pooling and billing</h3>
<p>Pooled plans share one data allowance across the fleet, so a busy device borrows from quiet ones instead of paying overage. Look for cost per device and SIM tags by customer or project, so you can bill your own customers from the same data. Plan fit matters as much as price: Butlr cut its connectivity management costs 40 percent by replacing carrier unlimited plans with plans sized to its sensors.</p>
<h3 id="6-apis-webhooks-and-integrations">6. APIs, webhooks and integrations</h3>
<p>Everything you can do in the console should work through a documented REST API, with webhooks that push events (activation, threshold breach, profile switch) into your ticketing, ERP or billing system. Ask for the API documentation before the demo.</p>
<h3 id="7-security-private-apn-vpn-and-zero-trust">7. Security: private APN, VPN and zero trust</h3>
<p>Keep devices off the public internet. A private APN, an IPsec VPN to your cloud, IMEI locking and per-group firewall rules shrink the attack surface of cellular IoT. Device credentials, firmware and application security still sit outside the CMP. Check who manages the APN and whether you can change rules yourself.</p>
<h3 id="8-automation-and-ai">8. Automation and AI</h3>
<p>Rules engines handle most automation: if a device crosses a threshold, suspend it and open a ticket. When a vendor says &#8220;AI&#8221;, ask what the model predicts and what it is allowed to change. Today it mostly means anomaly detection and usage forecasting. <a href="/spenza-product/spenza-mcp-server-ai/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Spenza&#8217;s MCP server</a> (79 tools on its Partner API) lets an AI assistant list SIMs, check usage and pause data, with you approving every write.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to choose an IoT CMP in 5 steps</h2>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Map the fleet.</strong> Countries, device count, data per device per month, module and eUICC models, and expected device life.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the platform type.</strong> A single-country fleet needs less than a 30-country OEM product.</li>
<li><strong>Send the same ten questions to three vendors.</strong> Networks and radio technologies per country; SGP.32 version and eIM; API coverage and documentation; largest bulk job; alerting and automation; pooling and billing exports; security options; SLA and support hours; exit terms for your SIMs and profiles; total 3-year price.</li>
<li><strong>Run a paid trial.</strong> Put 50 to 100 of your own devices in your hardest locations. Run one bulk policy change and one carrier switch. For each action, keep the request ID, the state you observed and when, and how it appears on the invoice.</li>
<li><strong>Model three-year cost.</strong> Include SIM hardware, data, overage, platform fees and field visits.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="faq">FAQs</h2>
<div class="mh-faq">
<details name="mh-faq" open><summary><h3 id="faq-1">What is an IoT connectivity management platform?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>An IoT connectivity management platform is software that activates, monitors, controls and bills the <a href="/esim/iot-sim-cards-guide/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SIM and eSIM connections</a> in a fleet of IoT devices, across one or many carriers. It replaces separate carrier portals with one console and an API.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-2">What is CMP in telecoms?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>In telecoms, CMP usually means connectivity management platform: the system an operator or enterprise uses to manage SIM states, rate plans, usage and alerts for IoT and M2M connections. Mobile operators offer one to their enterprise IoT customers, often built on Cisco IoT Control Center or Aeris IoT Accelerator.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-3">Who are the main IoT connectivity management platforms?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Widely used platforms include Cisco IoT Control Center, Aeris IoT Accelerator (formerly Ericsson), Soracom, KORE, floLIVE, emnify, Eseye, 1NCE, Onomondo, Hologram and Spenza. They differ by type: carrier-hosted, aggregator, own-core operator or operator-neutral eSIM platform.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-4">What are the key features of an IoT connectivity management platform?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Eight features matter most: SIM and eSIM lifecycle, multi-carrier access, bulk operations, usage alerts and anomaly detection, pooled rate plans and billing, a full REST API with webhooks, security controls such as private APN, and rules-based automation.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-5">How much does an IoT connectivity management platform cost per device?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Published prices range from a one-time $14 per SIM for 10 years and 500 MB (1NCE, US) to about $1 per SIM per month plus data (Hologram). In our 10,000-sensor example at 5 MB a month, that works out between $0.39 and $1.23 per device per month over three years.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-6">What is the difference between a CMP and a device management platform?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>A CMP manages the SIM and its network connection: activation, plans, usage and carrier profiles. A device management platform manages the device itself: firmware, configuration and reboots. Most fleets need both, connected through APIs.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3 id="faq-7">How does a CMP update or change policy for thousands of devices at once?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>It runs one job against a device group. You choose the group, set the new policy, plan or eSIM profile, test on a small batch and roll out in waves. Good platforms report a result per device; how failed devices are recovered depends on the platform, the carrier and the device.</p></div></details>
