<div id="spenza-merged-article" class="mh-article">
<p>Monetizing connectivity means selling the cellular service your devices and managed services already need as a recurring line item on your own invoice. For an MSP, that is a connectivity plan bundled with the hardware and support you deliver. For an OEM, it is a data plan embedded in the product. The model works when you control the plan, the price and the billing, rather than passing the customer to a carrier.</p>
<p>This guide covers where the revenue comes from, five strategies, how to bundle connectivity with hardware, what stops people from starting, and what to check before you launch. If you are comparing white-label routes in detail, our <a href="/telecom/telecom-msp-white-label-solutions-2025/">white-label MSP guide</a> goes deeper on that part.</p>
<p><em>Last verified 4 November 2026.</em></p>
<h2 id="why-connectivity">Why connectivity is worth selling</h2>
<p>A device sale or an IT project is revenue once. A connectivity plan is revenue every month for as long as the device is in service. That changes the economics in three ways.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Recurring income.</strong> Each connected device becomes a subscription, not a one-time invoice.</li><li><strong>Stickier customers.</strong> If you run the customer&#8217;s connectivity, you are part of their daily operations. Switching away means replacing more than a laptop supplier.</li><li><strong>A clearer offer.</strong> &#8220;Here are your devices, already connected, with one invoice and one team to call&#8221; is easier to buy than three vendor contracts.</li></ul>
<p>None of this is automatic. The recurring revenue only holds up if the plans are priced above your wholesale cost, the platform keeps billing accurate, and support does not eat the margin. We cover those checks below.</p>
<h2 id="five-strategies">Five strategies to monetize connectivity</h2>
<p>These are the five routes MSPs and OEMs use. Most businesses combine two or three.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Strategy</th><th>Who it fits</th><th>What you sell</th><th>Main risk</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Assess and bundle</td><td>MSPs with an installed base</td><td>Connectivity added to hardware or managed services clients already buy</td><td>Not knowing which clients actually have cellular devices</td></tr><tr><td>Embed connectivity</td><td>OEMs</td><td>A device that connects out of the box, through eSIM or a soldered SIM</td><td>Coverage and permanent-roaming limits in some countries</td></tr><tr><td>Sell under your brand</td><td>MSPs, OEMs, software companies</td><td>Plans and portal under your name, with a partner running the network side</td><td>Support expectations you must now meet</td></tr><tr><td>Tier the plans</td><td>OEMs, MSPs with mixed fleets</td><td>Plans matched to how a device is used, from low-power sensors to video</td><td>Overly complex catalogs that are hard to bill and support</td></tr><tr><td>Run it from one platform</td><td>Anyone past a few hundred SIMs</td><td>Provisioning, billing, usage and reporting in one place</td><td>Choosing a platform that cannot trace usage to invoice lines</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<h3 id="msp-play">For MSPs: become the wireless service manager</h3>
<p>Connectivity already runs through your clients&#8217; tablets, phones, routers and IoT endpoints. Someone else is billing for it. Start by mapping every cellular device in your managed base, who pays for the plan today and what it costs. That list shows the revenue you could capture and the clients where a bundle makes the most sense.</p>
<p>Then put a connectivity management platform in front of it, so SIMs, plans and usage are visible in one view. Package plans under your brand and add them to the monthly managed-service price. The goal is one charge for devices, support and connectivity, with usage alerts so clients are not surprised by overages. Our guide to <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">connectivity management platforms</a> lists what to compare.</p>
<h3 id="oem-play">For OEMs: turn the device into a service</h3>
<p>Every unit that leaves the factory can arrive connected. Provision an eSIM or a soldered SIM before shipping, so the customer does not have to find a carrier or insert a card. Then offer plans that fit how the product behaves: a small monthly allowance for a sensor, more data for a camera, a premium tier with analytics or monitoring.</p>
<p>The product decisions come first. Pick a form factor, decide which countries you will ship to and confirm that the module supports the bands you need. Our <a href="/esim/esim-for-oems/">eSIM for OEMs guide</a> walks through that, and the <a href="/telecom/trail-camera-connectivity-oem-guide/">trail camera OEM guide</a> shows the same logic applied to one device category.</p>
<h2 id="embedded-connectivity">Embedded connectivity as a product, not a feature</h2>
<p>For years connectivity was a hidden cost: the device worked, and the customer arranged the plan separately. Embedded connectivity changes that. The plan is part of what the customer buys, which means there is a billing relationship after the sale.</p>
<p>eSIM makes this practical because the operator profile is downloaded remotely instead of fixed in a plastic card. The GSMA publishes the <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/esim-specification/" rel="noopener">consumer and IoT eSIM specifications</a> that govern this, and SGP.32 is the version designed for IoT devices that have no screen or keyboard. Our <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 vs SGP.32 guide</a> explains which applies to your device.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/iot-sim-profile-swap-orange.webp" alt="A charcoal eUICC chip with gold contacts holds three fanned profile cards while a cream ribbon from a cell tower delivers one New profile card toward it." width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>An eSIM can receive a new operator profile remotely, so the same device can change plans without a new SIM card.</figcaption></figure>
<p>For your commercial model, the practical benefit is one hardware SKU. You do not stock different SIMs per country. You choose the profile when the device is activated or when it moves, and you can change it later if the commercial terms change. That also lowers your dependence on a single carrier, though you should check how much of that flexibility your agreement actually gives you. Our <a href="/esim/esim-orchestration-global-iot/">eSIM orchestration guide</a> covers the mechanics.</p>
<h2 id="bundle-with-hardware">Bundling connectivity with hardware</h2>
<p>This is the most direct MSP move, and it fixes a problem clients already feel. Consider an enterprise that orders 500 rugged tablets. You deliver and configure them. Then the client must find a carrier, buy 500 SIMs, arrange provisioning and fit connectivity into the device management workflow. When a unit goes offline, the client calls you, you point at the carrier, and the carrier points at the configuration. The client sits in the middle with no single owner.</p>
<p>Bundling closes that gap. The device arrives with a plan, you manage both, and one team is accountable.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Aspect</th><th>Client buys separately</th><th>You bundle it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Vendors</td><td>Hardware supplier plus carrier</td><td>One provider</td></tr><tr><td>Deployment</td><td>Wait for SIMs and provisioning</td><td>Devices arrive ready to connect</td></tr><tr><td>Accountability</td><td>Split between supplier and carrier</td><td>One owner for faults</td></tr><tr><td>Plan fit</td><td>Generic carrier plan</td><td>Plan chosen for the device&#8217;s job</td></tr><tr><td>Billing</td><td>Separate invoices</td><td>One monthly charge, or hardware-as-a-service plus connectivity</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Match the plan to the device. A logistics tracker that moves between regions needs multi-carrier coverage. A tablet streaming video needs more bandwidth. A sensor that reports a few kilobytes a day needs a low-power plan with a small allowance. Giving each class its own plan keeps both the client&#8217;s costs and your wholesale costs in line.</p>
<p>Price the bundle as one monthly figure or as hardware-as-a-service plus connectivity, whichever your clients find easier to approve. Connect billing to the PSA and RMM tools you already use where your platform supports that, and check the integration is real before you promise it.</p>
<h2 id="ways-to-resell">Three ways to resell: agent, white label or MVNO</h2>
<p>The objection from most MSPs is that they are not a telecom company. You do not need to become one. There are three routes, with different trade-offs.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Route</th><th>What you do</th><th>What you control</th><th>Effort</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Carrier agent</td><td>Refer clients to a carrier for a commission</td><td>Little. The carrier owns the customer, plan and invoice</td><td>Low</td></tr><tr><td>White label via a platform or MVNE</td><td>Sell plans under your brand on a partner&#8217;s telecom stack</td><td>Brand, pricing, bundling and the client relationship</td><td>Moderate</td></tr><tr><td>Own MVNO</td><td>Hold the carrier agreement and operate the stack</td><td>Everything, including wholesale terms</td><td>High, with commitments and regulatory work</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>The agent route is the lowest risk but the lowest value. You earn a referral fee, the carrier keeps the customer, and nothing you build adds to your own recurring revenue. Becoming an MVNO gives you the most control but requires carrier negotiation, a billing and provisioning stack and telecom expertise. Our <a href="/mvno/mvno-types/">MVNO types guide</a> and <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">roles guide</a> explain what each operator role involves.</p>
<p>White label sits in the middle. A platform or MVNE already has the carrier access and the operating stack, and you sell the service as your own. If you are weighing that route, see how a <a href="/mvno/white-label-mvno/">white-label MVNO</a> works, and what the <a href="/telecom/what-is-telecom-as-a-service-taas/">telecom-as-a-service model</a> covers.</p>
<h2 id="platform-checks">What to check in a platform before you sell</h2>
<p>Whichever route you choose, the platform decides whether the model is profitable. Ask for evidence on each of these.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Carrier access.</strong> Which networks, in which countries, under what terms, and what happens if one is removed?</li><li><strong>Branding.</strong> Can you run the portal and the plan names under your own brand?</li><li><strong>Billing trace.</strong> Can the vendor follow one known usage event to an invoice line and show how a correction is recorded? See our guide to <a href="/mvno/mvno-billing-platforms/">billing platforms</a>.</li><li><strong>Pooling and alerts.</strong> Can you share data across devices and warn clients before they go over?</li><li><strong>Integrations.</strong> Is there an API, and does it connect to your ticketing, PSA or CRM tools?</li><li><strong>eSIM management.</strong> Can profiles be added or changed remotely?</li><li><strong>Exit terms.</strong> Who owns the customer data and the SIM inventory if you leave?</li></ul>
<p>Spenza is one platform that offers these functions: a branded self-service experience for customers, custom plans, reseller billing and margin tracking, and an API. Check the current feature set against your requirements when you evaluate any vendor. Our <a href="/spenza-product/sell-devices-esims-shopify-spenza/">Shopify guide</a> shows one way to sell devices and eSIMs together online.</p>
<h2 id="build-the-offer">Build the offer in six steps</h2>
<ol><li><strong>Count the devices.</strong> List every cellular device in your base or product line, with its job and country.</li><li><strong>Group them.</strong> Sort by use: low-power sensors, mobile workers, video, travelers.</li><li><strong>Design a few plans.</strong> Three to five plans are easier to bill and support than twenty.</li><li><strong>Price from cost.</strong> Add wholesale, platform fee, support time and expected unused data, then your margin.</li><li><strong>Pilot with one client or product line.</strong> Include billing, an overage event and a fault ticket.</li><li><strong>Scale.</strong> Add tiers, analytics or other services once the basics hold.</li></ol>

<h2 id="pricing">Pricing the plans so the margin survives</h2>
<p>Margin is where most connectivity offers succeed or fail, and it is easy to get wrong because the costs arrive from several places. Work from your own numbers rather than an industry average, since wholesale rates, platform fees and support effort vary widely between providers and regions.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Wholesale cost per plan.</strong> The rate you pay per SIM or per megabyte, plus any minimum commitment.</li><li><strong>Platform fee.</strong> Per-SIM, per-month or revenue-share charges from the platform or MVNE.</li><li><strong>Support cost.</strong> The time your service desk spends on activation, faults and billing questions.</li><li><strong>Unused and overage data.</strong> Pooled data softens spikes. Set overage rules in writing so clients know what happens past the allowance.</li><li><strong>Hardware cycle.</strong> If you sell hardware-as-a-service, align the connectivity term with the device term so a plan does not outlive the device.</li></ul>
<p>Check the result with a worked example per plan: one month for a typical device, one for a heavy-use device and one for an idle device. If the idle device loses money, the plan needs a minimum charge or a lower allowance.</p>
<h2 id="post-sale">Services that sit on top of the connection</h2>
<p>Once connectivity is in place, OEMs and MSPs can charge for what the connection makes possible. Examples include remote monitoring, usage analytics, device health alerts, extended warranties and managed security. These are separate products with their own pricing, and they often matter more over time than the data itself because customers value outcomes more than megabytes. Keep them optional at first, so the base bundle stays simple to quote and to compare against a carrier plan.</p>
<h2 id="risks">Risks to plan for</h2>
<ul><li><strong>Support load.</strong> Once you sell connectivity, the client expects you to fix it. Agree how faults are triaged between you and the carrier side before launch.</li><li><strong>Coverage promises.</strong> Do not promise a country until you have checked plan terms and local rules. Permanent roaming is restricted in some markets.</li><li><strong>Billing disputes.</strong> Usage and invoices need to match. Test this before real money moves.</li><li><strong>Unused data.</strong> Pools help, but a plan allowance nobody uses is margin you may not keep, or margin you may over-promise.</li><li><strong>Lock-in.</strong> A single-carrier dependence can limit your options later, so read the terms on switching.</li></ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Connectivity is a product you can sell, not just a cost that sits on someone else&#8217;s bill. MSPs can bundle it with hardware and managed services. OEMs can ship it inside the device. Either way, the work is the same: know the devices, design a small set of plans, price from your real costs and choose a platform that traces usage through to the invoice.</p>
<p>Start with one segment and one pilot. If the numbers hold and support stays manageable, extend from there. Related reading includes our <a href="/esim/travel-esim-solutions-monetize-devices/">guide to monetizing devices with eSIM</a>.</p>
