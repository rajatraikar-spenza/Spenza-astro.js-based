<p>There are five commonly cited types of MVNO: the branded reseller (also called skinny), the thin MVNO, the light MVNO, the thick MVNO and the full MVNO. They differ in one thing: how much of the operating stack the brand owns itself rather than buys from a host network or an enabler. A branded reseller owns the brand and the sale. A light MVNO also runs its own plans, billing and customer care. A full MVNO adds its own core network and leases only the radio network.</p>
<p>The names are an industry convention, not a standard, so two suppliers can use &#8220;light&#8221; for different packages. This guide compares the five types, goes deeper on the light and full models, explains how to choose, and covers the alternatives to launching an MVNO at all: reselling, managed services and connectivity resale. For who plays which part in the chain, see <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">MNO, MVNO, MVNE and MVNA roles compared</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-an-mvno">What an MVNO is, and why types exist</h2>
<p>A mobile virtual network operator sells mobile service under its own brand over a radio network it does not own. A host mobile network operator (MNO) carries the traffic. The MVNO decides who it sells to, what plans look like and how the customer is served. Our overview of <a href="/mvno/mvno/">what an MVNO is</a> covers the basics; this page is about the differences between models.</p>
<p>Types exist because &#8220;MVNO&#8221; covers very different businesses. Some are a brand and a price list. Others run subscriber databases, charging systems and their own SIM profiles. Regulators and standards bodies describe the split in similar terms. Ofcom, in its April 2025 statement on global titles, separates MVNOs that have their own core network equipment (&#8220;often referred to as thick MVNOs or full MVNOs&#8221;) from those that do not (&#8220;often referred to as thin or light MVNOs&#8221;), and notes that enablers and aggregators can supply the second group (<a href="https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/consultations/category-1-10-weeks/185679-consultation-global-titles-and-mobile-network-security/associated-documents/statement-global-titles-and-mobile-network-security.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ofcom statement</a>). The ITU&#8217;s 2021 report on telecom economics uses a four-way split: reseller, service provider, light and full (<a href="https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-d/opb/stg/D-STG-SG01.04.2-2021-PDF-E.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ITU-D Question 4/1 output report</a>, section 4.1).</p>
<h2 id="types-compared">The five MVNO types compared</h2>
<p>Read the table as a ladder. Each step up gives the brand more control over price, product and customer data, and asks for more systems, people and compliance work in return. The naming labels in the second column are the closest equivalents in other classifications, not exact matches.</p>

<p>Where the ITU and Ofcom classifications are available, they agree on the shape. The ITU describes a reseller MVNO as one that does not own the subscriber, the infrastructure or the SIM cards. A service provider MVNO may own subscriber management, application and billing platforms, SIM cards and tariffs. A light MVNO adds ownership of the customer and of the intelligent network platform, and partial ownership of the value-added services platform. A full MVNO owns the core network of a mobile operator and must provide the required network performance and quality of service on it. The ITU report names local and ethnic operators as typical light MVNOs. That wording is its own, and the five-way split used here is the more common industry shorthand.</p>
<h2 id="branded-reseller">Branded reseller (skinny MVNO)</h2>
<p>A branded reseller sells a host&#8217;s service under its own name. The host or an enabler provisions SIMs, rates usage, bills and usually supplies the care tooling. The brand owns marketing, sales and the relationship it builds through them. Plan design is limited to what the host will wholesale.</p>
<p><strong>Strengths:</strong> the lightest commitment of any type, no billing or core platform to run, and a quick way to learn whether your audience buys mobile service. <strong>Weaknesses:</strong> little room to differentiate, since competitors on the same host can look alike, and your roadmap follows the host&#8217;s. It suits retailers, consumer brands and software companies that already own an audience and want to cross-sell connectivity. Check how much customer data the arrangement actually lets you keep, because the definitions differ between suppliers.</p>
<h2 id="thin-mvno">Thin MVNO (service provider)</h2>
<p>A thin MVNO looks like a reseller with a bigger share of the customer relationship. It sets its own prices and promotions, usually runs its own customer service, and may handle basic billing functions. The core network and the technical operation stay with the host or an enabler. This is a common model for operators serving a defined community, such as diaspora callers, or a regional provider adding mobile to broadband. The advantages over a reseller are control of branding and pricing and the ability to change offers quickly. The limits are the same dependence on the host for anything technical and no network-level features.</p>
<h2 id="light-mvno">Light MVNO: the detail</h2>
<p>A light MVNO is defined by what it runs for itself on the business side. It operates its own BSS: billing, subscription management, product catalog and customer care, and often its own CRM. For the OSS and BSS split see <a href="/telecom/oss-bss/">OSS and BSS in telecom</a>. It does not own the core network, so authentication, routing and radio access come from the host, often through an MVNE that exposes them behind a single integration.</p>
<h3 id="light-what-you-control">What a light MVNO controls</h3>
<ul>
<li>Plans, pricing, bundles and promotions, without waiting for the host to publish them.</li>
<li>Billing and subscriptions, including usage-based and recurring models.</li>
<li>Customer care, self-service and the customer record.</li>
<li>Brand, channels and extra services such as streaming partnerships, banking features or IoT connectivity.</li>
<li>Often the SIM or eSIM design and the activation journey. Whether a light MVNO issues its own profiles depends on the enabler and the host agreement.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="light-what-you-depend-on">What it depends on</h3>
<ul>
<li>Coverage, network quality and upgrade timing, which belong to the host.</li>
<li>Wholesale pricing. Your margin is whatever remains between wholesale rates and what customers pay, so the host contract is the most important commercial document you will sign.</li>
<li>The host&#8217;s own retail offers, which compete with yours. Contract terms around exclusivity and notice periods matter here.</li>
<li>Compliance in each market. Whether a license, registration or other approval applies is a national question, not a property of the type.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="light-use-cases">Where light MVNOs work</h3>
<p>Typical light MVNOs focus on a defined audience (seniors, students, travelers, expatriates), extend an existing brand with a mobile product, or sell connectivity inside a device or IoT offer. Businesses deploying connected devices should treat IoT as its own design problem, since provisioning, lifecycle and fleet billing differ from consumer plans. eSIM has also lowered the cost of the activation journey for light operators, because the customer can install a profile without waiting for a card in the post.</p>
<h3 id="light-vs-others">Light MVNO versus the neighbors</h3>
<div class="mh-table" tabindex="0" role="region" aria-label="Table 2: Light MVNO against reseller and full"><table><thead><tr><th scope="col"><strong>Aspect</strong></th><th scope="col"><strong>Branded reseller</strong></th><th scope="col"><strong>Light MVNO</strong></th><th scope="col"><strong>Full MVNO</strong></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><strong>Network core</strong></td><td>Host</td><td>Host</td><td>Own</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Plans and pricing</strong></td><td>Limited to host plans</td><td>Own</td><td>Own</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Billing and care</strong></td><td>Host or enabler</td><td>Own, on an enabler platform or own stack</td><td>Own</td></tr><tr><td><strong>SIM and eSIM profiles</strong></td><td>Host</td><td>Varies by agreement</td><td>Own</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Customer data</strong></td><td>Shared with or held by host</td><td>Owned in the BSS</td><td>Owned, including network-side data</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Technical skills needed</strong></td><td>Minimal</td><td>Product, billing and care operations</td><td>Telecom engineering and network operations</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Regulatory exposure</strong></td><td>Lowest</td><td>Depends on the market</td><td>Highest</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>A common misreading is that a light MVNO cannot use advanced network features. Features such as 5G slicing or IoT eSIM management depend on what the host network exposes and what the platform supports, not on the label. A light MVNO can use them if the host and platform provide them. A full MVNO simply has more direct control over the functions involved.</p>
<h2 id="thick-mvno">Thick MVNO (partial core)</h2>
<p>A thick MVNO runs part of the core. That may be a subscriber server, a data gateway or a policy and filtering function, while the radio network and the rest of the core stay with the host. Owning those elements allows custom data routing, zero-rated applications, traffic handling and richer analytics than a light MVNO can reach through a host&#8217;s APIs. The cost is real telecom engineering, interconnection work with the host and a heavier compliance profile in most markets. Some sources treat thick and full as the same model. Ofcom does, using the pairing &#8220;thick MVNOs or full MVNOs&#8221;. Either way, plan for core operations once you take any part of it.</p>
<h2 id="full-mvno">Full MVNO: the detail</h2>
<p>A full MVNO owns or operates the core network, including subscriber databases, authentication, charging and billing, SIM and eSIM profile issuance and its own roaming and interconnect arrangements, and leases only the radio access network from one or more hosts. It does not own towers. A 5G standalone core is a strong sign of this model because it puts core functions, subscriber management and policy in the operator&#8217;s hands, but it is not by itself the definition. What defines a full MVNO is control of the key core elements and the identity of the subscriber.</p>
<h3 id="full-what-it-owns">What a full MVNO owns</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>SIM issuance.</strong> It creates its own SIM and eSIM profiles, which allows customized profiles, remote provisioning and its own fraud controls.</li>
<li><strong>Billing and care.</strong> It defines pay-as-you-go, subscription, bundle and enterprise models, and manages the customer relationship end to end.</li>
<li><strong>Subscriber management and authentication.</strong> It controls data, quality of service rules and how subscribers move between networks.</li>
<li><strong>Network relationships.</strong> It can contract with more than one host, which supports multi-network coverage and roaming strategies.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="full-benefits">Benefits and the price of them</h3>
<p>The benefits follow from ownership: control of the product, the brand and the data, room to sign several hosts and expand into new markets, and a stronger negotiating position. The costs follow from the same ownership. You build and run core systems, pass the compliance tests of each market, negotiate and manage host agreements, hire for operations and security, and acquire customers without a host&#8217;s brand behind you. The risks that most often damage a full MVNO launch are underestimating infrastructure cost, picking the wrong wholesale partner, waiting on approvals, weak billing integration and thin onboarding. Many of these are covered in <a href="/mvno/biggest-mvno-mistakes-how-to-avoid-2026/">common MVNO launch mistakes</a>.</p>
<p>It is also worth testing the premise. Full control is valuable when you have a product that depends on it, such as a multi-country eSIM service, enterprise or IoT connectivity with special data paths, or an operator launching a sub-brand. For a brand whose edge is distribution and customer service, a lighter model can deliver the same product sooner. Work out cost with <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">MVNO launch cost</a> before committing to the heavier model; it depends too much on market and scope to give a single range here.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-choose">How to choose the right type</h2>
<p>Choose by answering four questions in order. The answers usually point to one step on the ladder.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>What do you need to control?</strong> If only the brand, the offer and the sale, a reseller or thin model is enough. If billing logic, product catalog and the customer record, you are in light MVNO territory. If SIM issuance, routing or network data, you are moving to thick or full.</li>
<li><strong>What can you run?</strong> Billing and care operations are a different skill from network engineering. Be honest about the team you have and the platform you would rent. An enabler can take over much of the technical load; see <a href="/mvno/how-mvnes-accelerate-mvno-launch/">how MVNEs accelerate an MVNO launch</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Who is the customer?</strong> Mass-market price buyers favor a lean model with sharp pricing. Communities, travelers and enterprise or IoT buyers value custom plans, eSIM journeys and data controls that a light or full model supports.</li>
<li><strong>What can you commit?</strong> Compare launch scope, run costs and host terms for each option rather than quoting industry averages. Time to launch and spend vary far more with market and supplier than with type.</li>
</ol>

<h3 id="growth-path">Moving up the ladder</h3>
<p>Brands often start lighter and move up as volume and capability grow, for example from branded reseller to light MVNO to full MVNO. That path works only if the platform you start on can carry you: a stack built for reseller operations that cannot handle your own billing logic forces a migration just when you are growing. Ask any provider how plans, subscribers and identifiers move when you change model, and how SIM or eSIM profiles are handled in the move.</p>
<h2 id="alternatives">Alternatives to launching an MVNO</h2>
<p>Not every company that wants to sell connectivity needs to become an operator. Three simpler models cover most of what non-telecom businesses actually want. They are routes to revenue, not MVNO types, and they carry the same warning as the rest of this guide: the label tells you little, so read what the agreement covers.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Value-added reseller.</strong> You sell connectivity plans bundled with your own product, such as hardware, software or services. A platform or carrier supplies the plans and often the billing. Your investment is mostly commercial.</li>
<li><strong>Managed service provider.</strong> You run telecom services for business customers: sourcing plans, monitoring usage, handling billing queries and supporting end users, usually with an expense-management tool. The value is the service, not the network.</li>
<li><strong>IoT connectivity reseller.</strong> You ship SIMs or eSIMs with devices and manage the connections centrally, which suits device makers, fleet operators and telematics providers.</li>
</ul>
<p>The main reason to pick one of these over a branded reseller or light MVNO is how much of the customer experience you want to own. A reseller sells someone else&#8217;s plan alongside your product. A branded reseller or light MVNO sells a mobile service under a brand of its own.</p>
<p>Two other routes shape the same decision. A mobile virtual network aggregator (MVNA) holds the wholesale relationship with one or more host networks and passes access to many MVNOs, which can help a small brand with weaker bargaining power. An MVNE supplies the operating platform instead. They solve different problems, and the <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">roles guide</a> explains when you need which. If you want your own brand without negotiating with a host, a white label arrangement is the usual answer; see <a href="/mvno/white-label-mvno/">white label MVNO scope, ownership and cost</a>. If you are targeting the United States, the <a href="/mvno/launch-mvno-us-guide/">US MVNO launch guide</a> covers the regulatory side, and the <a href="/mvno/launch-mvno-7-days/">fast-launch guide</a> shows what a compressed schedule looks like in practice.</p>
<h2 id="where-spenza-fits">Where a platform fits</h2>
<p>The type you choose and the platform you run on are separate decisions. Spenza is a platform provider rather than a network operator, so it sits on the enabler side of the chain, supplying the systems a brand runs on rather than the network itself. A useful test of any platform is whether it can support more than one step on the ladder, so that moving from a reseller to a light MVNO does not mean re-platforming. Brands considering that route can see a worked example in the <a href="/spenza-product/rocketphone-case-study/">RocketPhone case study</a>.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>MVNO types are a spectrum of ownership. A branded reseller owns the brand and the sale; a thin MVNO adds pricing and care; a light MVNO adds billing, subscriptions and the customer record; thick and full MVNOs add parts or all of the core network. More control brings more cost, more operational work and more compliance, so choose the lightest model that gives you what your product needs and make sure the platform can grow with you. If you only want to sell connectivity alongside another product, reselling or a managed service may be enough. Whichever you pick, read the scope in the agreement, not the label on the package.</p>
