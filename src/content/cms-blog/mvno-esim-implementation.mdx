<p><em>Last verified 2 November 2026.</em></p>
<p>An MVNO implements eSIM by connecting its own systems to a GSMA-compliant remote SIM provisioning (RSP) chain. In practice that means five jobs: pick or contract an SM-DP+ (the server that prepares and delivers profiles), get eSIM profiles from your host network, wire your order and billing systems to the SM-DP+ so each sale creates a profile, deliver an activation code to the customer by QR code, app or push, and handle the lifecycle afterwards: replacement, suspension, plan change and termination. The phone side, the eUICC and the LPA that downloads the profile, already ships in the device.</p>
<p>This guide walks through those steps in the order an MVNO team meets them: the pieces involved, the decisions to make first, the activation flow, delivery methods, integration with BSS and OSS, security, testing, and migrating subscribers who hold a plastic SIM today. Protocol details come from the GSMA consumer RSP specification, <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/SGP.22-v2.7-1.docx" rel="noopener">GSMA SGP.22 v2.7</a>. For the standards background, see our guides to <a href="/esim/what-is-remote-sim-provisioning-rsp-guide-2026/">remote SIM provisioning</a> and <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 versus SGP.32</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-you-need">What does an MVNO need to run eSIM?</h2>
<p>Five components have to work together. Only some of them are yours to build or buy.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Component</th><th>What it does</th><th>Who normally provides it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>eUICC and LPA</td><td>The eUICC is the secure chip in the device that holds profiles. The LPA (Local Profile Assistant) is the on-device software that talks to the SM-DP+ and installs a profile.</td><td>The device maker. You test against devices; you do not ship these.</td></tr><tr><td>SM-DP+</td><td>Subscription Manager Data Preparation plus. It generates, protects and stores profiles and delivers each one to the single eUICC it is bound to.</td><td>You contract it, or your MVNE or platform provider runs it. Operators can also run their own.</td></tr><tr><td>SM-DS</td><td>A discovery server. It lets a device learn that a profile is waiting for it, without a code being typed or scanned.</td><td>A GSMA-defined service your SM-DP+ registers events with. Optional for most MVNO designs.</td></tr><tr><td>Host network profile source</td><td>The subscription data (IMSI, keys, network settings) that becomes the profile content.</td><td>Your host MNO, directly or through your MVNE.</td></tr><tr><td>BSS and OSS</td><td>Catalog, orders, customer records, billing, and the activation and provisioning systems that call the SM-DP+.</td><td>You, your MVNE, or a platform vendor. See the <a href="/telecom/oss-bss/">OSS and BSS guide</a>.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>The SM-DP+ is the piece that surprises new operators. A plastic SIM is personalized at a card factory and posted. With eSIM, the same personalization happens in software at order time, on a server that holds your subscribers&#8217; credentials in protected form. That makes the choice of who runs it a security and contractual decision as much as a technical one.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Roles check.</strong> If you are a light MVNO, the MVNE or platform you build on may already operate the SM-DP+ link and expose it through one API. Confirm that in writing. The split between MNO, MVNO, MVNE and MVNA responsibilities is covered in <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">what an MVNE is and how the roles differ</a>.</p></div>
<h2 id="decisions-first">What should you decide before you integrate anything?</h2>
<p>Most delays in eSIM projects trace back to questions nobody answered in week one. Settle these first.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Does your host network support eSIM profiles?</strong> eSIM needs the network to issue profiles for your brand and to accept them in its provisioning chain. Some hosts support it fully, some charge for it, some do not offer it yet. This is a commercial question for your MNO agreement, so raise it before you commit to a launch date. Our <a href="/mvno/mvno-types/">guide to MVNO types</a> shows how much of this you control under each model.</li>
<li><strong>Who runs the SM-DP+?</strong> Run your own, contract one from a provider, or use your MVNE&#8217;s. Operating your own means taking on the security accreditation, uptime and key-management work that comes with it. GSMA runs accreditation under its Security Accreditation Scheme, and subscription management providers are assessed under its SAS-SM branch; ask any provider for its current status.</li>
<li><strong>Consumer, IoT or both?</strong> Consumer phones follow SGP.22, where the user&#8217;s device drives the download. Unattended IoT devices follow a different model under SGP.32, with a remote manager in the loop. If you plan to serve both, check that your platform handles both architectures. The <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">standards comparison</a> and our guide to <a href="/esim/iot-esim-providers/">IoT eSIM providers</a> explain the difference.</li>
<li><strong>Which devices will you support?</strong> eSIM needs a device with an eUICC and a compatible LPA. Decide on a supported-device list and a method to check eligibility at checkout, so a customer on an unsupported handset learns that before paying.</li>
<li><strong>eSIM-only, or eSIM alongside plastic?</strong> A new MVNO can launch eSIM-first and keep physical SIMs as a fallback. An established one has the migration problem covered later in this article.</li>
<li><strong>Which regulations apply?</strong> Identity checks at activation, data-protection rules for stored subscriber data and local telecom registration rules all attach to the digital flow just as they did to the shop counter. Your legal team should map them to the new steps.</li>
</ul>
<p>Launch cost and timing depend heavily on these answers; the <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">MVNO launch cost guide</a> and <a href="/mvno/how-mvnes-accelerate-mvno-launch/">how MVNEs accelerate launch</a> cover the build-versus-rent trade-offs.</p>
<h2 id="activation-flow">How does the eSIM activation flow work end to end?</h2>
<p>The flow has two halves. First your systems ask the SM-DP+ to prepare a profile (the operator side, called ES2+ in the specification). Then the customer&#8217;s device downloads it (the device side, ES9+ between the LPA and the SM-DP+). Knowing which half failed is most of what you need for support.</p>
<h3 id="operator-side">Operator side: ordering the profile</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Download order.</strong> When a customer buys, your system calls <code>ES2+.DownloadOrder</code>. The SM-DP+ reserves an ICCID from its inventory for the requested profile type. The device&#8217;s EID can be supplied now, or later if it is not known yet.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm order.</strong> <code>ES2+.ConfirmOrder</code> confirms the ICCID allocation, generates a MatchingID if you did not provide one, and stores the EID if available. If you require a confirmation code, the SM-DP+ stores a hash of it.</li>
<li><strong>Back-end provisioning.</strong> You activate the subscription in your own core, billing and the host network&#8217;s systems. The specification expects this to finish before the customer can download.</li>
<li><strong>Release profile.</strong> <code>ES2+.ReleaseProfile</code> sets the profile to released so the download can begin. The step is there precisely so the customer cannot install a profile before the network knows about it.</li>
<li><strong>Progress callbacks.</strong> As the download proceeds, the SM-DP+ calls your <code>ES2+.HandleDownloadProgressInfo</code> endpoint. Use these callbacks to move the order through states in your own system.</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="device-side">Device side: downloading and installing</h3>
<p>The LPA starts from one of three sources. It reads an activation code (typed or scanned), it retrieves a pending event from an SM-DS, or it uses a default SM-DP+ address stored on the eUICC. It then contacts the SM-DP+ over ES9+, the two sides authenticate each other with certificates, and the SM-DP+ checks eligibility using the device and eUICC information. If a confirmation code is required, the user enters it. The SM-DP+ then delivers a bound profile package, cryptographically tied to that one eUICC, which the eUICC installs. Notifications go back to the SM-DP+ and onward to your systems so you can see that the profile is installed and later enabled.</p>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/esim-order-flow-orange.webp" alt="A phone showing a QR code beside a shopping basket, a charcoal server cabinet with a gear, and a second phone with an active signal, joined by cream ribbons and labelled Order, Provision and Active" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The eSIM order path: your systems order a profile, the SM-DP+ prepares it, the device downloads it and the network recognizes it.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Two states trip teams up. A profile can be <em>installed</em> but not <em>enabled</em>; the customer may need to switch the line on in device settings. And a download can fail halfway, leaving a reserved ICCID with no live service. Define in advance whether a failed order is retried, cancelled with <code>ES2+.CancelOrder</code>, or sent to manual review, and make sure billing starts only on the state you intend. If you manage many carriers or profile types from one console, an orchestration layer helps; see <a href="/esim/esim-orchestration-global-iot/">eSIM orchestration</a>.</p>
<h2 id="delivery-methods">How do customers receive their eSIM: QR code, app or push?</h2>
<p>The activation code is what lets a device find the right SM-DP+ and the right order. Its format is fixed by the specification: the string <code>1$</code>, then the SM-DP+ address, then <code>$</code> and the MatchingID, with optional fields for the SM-DP+ OID and a confirmation-code flag. When it is encoded in a QR code it carries the prefix <code>LPA:</code>. The maximum length is 255 characters. A manual-entry version exists for when scanning is not possible.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Delivery method</th><th>How it works</th><th>Best for</th><th>Watch for</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>QR code on screen or email</td><td>The customer scans the code in device settings, and the LPA reads the activation code.</td><td>Web checkout, and customers moving the plan to a second device.</td><td>Codes shown on the same phone that needs to scan them. Offer the manual code as a fallback.</td></tr><tr><td>Manual activation code</td><td>The customer types the SM-DP+ address and MatchingID.</td><td>Support calls and accessibility.</td><td>Typing errors. Keep codes short and avoid ambiguous characters in your MatchingID scheme.</td></tr><tr><td>App-driven install</td><td>Your app (or the device&#8217;s carrier flow, where the device maker supports one) passes the code to the LPA without scanning.</td><td>A smoother journey for existing app users.</td><td>Depends on what each device maker allows. Test each target platform and OS version yourself.</td></tr><tr><td>SM-DS discovery</td><td>The SM-DP+ registers an event with the discovery server; the device finds it and offers the download.</td><td>Cases where the EID is known and you want no code handling.</td><td>Needs the EID at order time and an SM-DS route. More moving parts.</td></tr><tr><td>Default SM-DP+ on the eUICC</td><td>The eUICC holds a default SM-DP+ address and the LPA contacts it.</td><td>Pre-provisioned or bulk-deployed devices.</td><td>Requires control over what is preloaded on the eUICC.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/mvno-esim-delivery-paths-orange.webp" alt="A charcoal SM-DP+ server cabinet with a gear sends three cream ribbon paths to a smartphone with a gold star, with a QR code card above, a signpost labelled SM-DS in the middle and a small cell tower below." width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Three common ways a profile reaches a handset: a scanned code, a discovery signpost and a preloaded default.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Treat the activation code like a credential. It lets whoever holds it download a profile that was ordered for a customer. Make codes single-use, expire unused ones, send them only to the verified account holder, and consider a confirmation code for higher-risk sales. Never print the same code on packaging that many people handle.</p>
<h2 id="bss-oss-integration">How does eSIM connect to billing, CRM and the network?</h2>
<p>The SM-DP+ knows about profiles. It does not know about customers, plans or invoices. Your integration layer must hold that mapping and keep it consistent across systems.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>One subscription, many identifiers.</strong> A customer&#8217;s commercial subscription, the MSISDN, the ICCID of the profile and the EID of the device are separate. Decide which one is the anchor. An eSIM replacement on a new phone should change the ICCID and EID under the same subscription, not open a second billable line.</li>
<li><strong>Order events drive billing.</strong> Start charging on the activation state you defined, not on payment or on profile download alone. The <a href="/mvno/mvno-billing-platforms/">MVNO billing platform guide</a> describes tests for tying lifecycle events to invoices.</li>
<li><strong>Real-time status in customer care.</strong> Agents need to see whether a profile is ordered, released, downloaded, installed or enabled, and be able to cancel and reissue without a ticket to engineering.</li>
<li><strong>API-first where possible.</strong> The more of these steps are API calls, the easier it is to add self-service and automation. A connectivity platform that exposes provisioning, SIM state and usage through one API shortens this work considerably.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="security">What security controls does an eSIM rollout need?</h2>
<p>The main protections sit inside the standard: profiles are encrypted and bound to one eUICC, and the device and server authenticate each other with certificates. Your responsibility is everything around that.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Identity at order time.</strong> Verify the account holder before issuing a code, and again before a replacement. SIM-swap fraud moves from the shop counter to the web form.</li>
<li><strong>Access to the operator API.</strong> The ES2+ credentials can order profiles. Restrict them, rotate them and log every call.</li>
<li><strong>Code handling.</strong> Single-use codes, short expiry, no codes in logs or analytics, delivery only to verified channels.</li>
<li><strong>Supplier assurance.</strong> Ask your SM-DP+ provider for its GSMA accreditation status and its latest audit evidence. Run your own penetration tests on the ordering and delivery paths that you control.</li>
</ul>
<p>The specification also limits retries: an SM-DP+ can stop a download after a maximum number of attempts, and the same applies to confirmation-code attempts. Check the limits your provider has set and what customer care does when a customer hits them.</p>
<h2 id="testing">How should you test before launch?</h2>
<p>Build a test matrix before the first customer. Device behavior, not your own code, usually causes the surprises.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Happy path on real handsets.</strong> Order, deliver a code, install, enable, make a call, use data, for each supported device family and OS version. Include at least one low-end and one older model.</li>
<li><strong>Failure paths.</strong> Expired code, wrong confirmation code, download interrupted, device offline, profile already used, unsupported device. Check what the customer sees and what your system records.</li>
<li><strong>Lifecycle.</strong> Replacement on a new phone, suspend, resume, plan change and terminate, each tied to the right billing outcome.</li>
<li><strong>Test profiles.</strong> Use test eUICCs or a test kit with trial profiles from your provider, so engineering can iterate without consuming live inventory.</li>
<li><strong>Host-network acceptance.</strong> Confirm that a profile released by the SM-DP+ is actually recognized by the network and the HLR/HSS or equivalent, including roaming behavior.</li>
</ul>
<p>Allow enough calendar time for third parties. Integration time depends on your provider&#8217;s API, how many carriers are involved, your own release cycle and the host network&#8217;s certification steps, so ask for a plan with named dependencies rather than a single duration.</p>
<h2 id="migration">How do you migrate existing physical SIM subscribers to eSIM?</h2>
<p>Migrate in waves, not in a switch-over. New customers on eSIM-capable devices are the safe starting point; existing subscribers follow when they choose to or when they replace a device.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Start with new sales.</strong> Offer eSIM at checkout to customers with eligible devices, keep plastic as the fallback.</li>
<li><strong>Add self-service swap.</strong> Let existing customers convert from physical SIM to eSIM in the app after identity verification. The old SIM stops working when the new profile is enabled.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a plastic option.</strong> Not every device in use supports eSIM, and rugged or older equipment often does not. Plan to run both for years rather than forcing a cutover.</li>
<li><strong>Educate customers.</strong> Scanning a code in settings is new to many people. Short instructions in the app, an email and the support script reduce abandoned activations.</li>
</ol>
<figure class="wp-block-image"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/mvno-esim-migration-orange.webp" alt="A stack of physical SIM cards on the left and a smartphone on the right joined by a cream ribbon that carries three smaller SIM cards, labelled Physical SIM and eSIM." width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Migration runs in stages: new sales first, then self-service swaps, with physical SIMs kept for devices that cannot take a profile.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Measure the migration with a few simple numbers: the share of new activations on eSIM, the activation completion rate, the number of support contacts per activation, and the count of lost or replaced SIMs. These show whether the change is paying off without relying on industry forecasts.</p>
<h2 id="business-case">What does eSIM change for costs and customer experience?</h2>
<p>Digital delivery removes SIM manufacturing, packaging, inventory and postage from each sale, and it removes the days a customer used to wait. It also lets you test plans faster, because a new plan is a catalog change rather than a new card run. Your own numbers should drive the business case: take your current cost per SIM, shipping, failed-delivery rate and dealer commission, and compare them with the SM-DP+ fees, integration cost and support effort on the eSIM side. The <a href="/mvno/mvno-launch-cost/">launch cost guide</a> lists the line items.</p>
<p>New capabilities follow from the same foundation: travelers can add a local plan without a new card, and a customer can hold more than one profile on one device. Whether those become products depends on your host network agreements and on how much your platform can automate. For 5G-specific services and network slicing, the constraint is your host&#8217;s offer rather than the eSIM layer, so confirm that with your MNO.</p>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>eSIM implementation for an MVNO is mainly an integration project. The standard defines how a profile is prepared, protected and delivered, so your work lies in the decisions around it: who runs the SM-DP+, whether your host network will issue profiles, how an order moves from checkout to a released profile, how the activation code reaches the right person, and how billing and care stay in step with each profile state. Settle those first, test on real devices and failure cases, migrate in waves, and keep plastic SIMs available until the devices that need them are gone.</p>
