<div id="spenza-merged-article" class="mh-article">
<p>The short answer: IoT connectivity in healthcare is the network layer that moves readings and alerts from a medical or monitoring device to the people and systems that act on them, and for remote patient monitoring (RPM) it is judged on one thing, whether data arrives when it should. That means planning for weak coverage, carrier outages, network shutdowns and regulated data from the start, not after the first missed reading.</p>
<p>This guide covers how RPM connectivity is put together, the ways a single-carrier SIM fails, how multi-carrier eSIM and a connectivity management platform address that, what changes for elder-care medical alert devices, and which security and compliance questions to put to a vendor. It is general information, not legal or medical advice. Rules differ by country and by product, so have your counsel and regulatory advisers review any decision that depends on them.</p>
<p><em>Last verified 2 November 2026.</em></p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>Key takeaway:</strong> design the connection so that no single carrier, network technology or SIM profile is a single point of failure, then test it where patients actually live.</p></div>
<h2 id="rpm-connectivity-layers">How RPM connectivity is put together</h2>
<p>Remote patient monitoring moves data in a chain. A sensor or device captures a reading. A connection carries it off the device. A cloud or on-premises system stores it and applies rules. Software shows it to a clinician or care team, and may raise an alert. Only the second link is connectivity, but every other link depends on it.</p>
<figure><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthcare-rpm-connectivity-chain-orange.webp" alt="A home monitoring device sends a signal through a cell tower to a cloud server and on to a clinic dashboard, with a second tower as a backup path" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>From the home to the care team: the connection in the middle is the link that most often decides whether the chain works.</figcaption></figure>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Layer</th><th>What it does</th><th>What to ask about connectivity</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Device</td><td>Captures the reading and holds it until it can be sent</td><td>Does it store readings if offline? Which radio technologies does the module support?</td></tr><tr><td>Connection</td><td>Carries data off the device by Wi-Fi, a hub or a cellular SIM</td><td>Which networks can it reach? What happens when the first one fails?</td></tr><tr><td>Platform</td><td>Receives, stores and routes data</td><td>Where is data stored, and who can access it?</td></tr><tr><td>Application</td><td>Shows data and raises alerts to the care team</td><td>How fast must an alert arrive, and who is told if a device goes silent?</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Two design decisions come first. The first is where the connection lives: in the device itself, or in a hub that devices pair with. The second is whether patients are asked to supply their own network. A hub that relies on the patient&#8217;s home Wi-Fi is cheap, but it fails when the router is switched off, the password changes or the patient moves. A device with its own cellular connection removes that dependency, and it is why many programs ship devices that work out of the box.</p>
<h2 id="connectivity-options">Connectivity options for medical and monitoring devices</h2>
<p>No option is best everywhere. The right choice depends on how much data the device sends, how quickly an alert must arrive, whether it moves, and how much setup you can ask of the patient.</p>
<div class="mh-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Option</th><th>Strengths</th><th>Limits</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Patient Wi-Fi</td><td>No connectivity cost to the program; high bandwidth</td><td>Depends on the patient&#8217;s router and setup; fails when the household network changes</td></tr><tr><td>Bluetooth to a phone or hub</td><td>Low power; works with common consumer devices</td><td>Needs a second device nearby to be online; adds a failure point</td></tr><tr><td>Cellular, single-carrier SIM</td><td>Works without patient setup</td><td>Inherits one carrier&#8217;s coverage gaps, outages and shutdown schedule</td></tr><tr><td>Cellular, multi-carrier SIM or eSIM</td><td>Can move to another network; one device model for many regions</td><td>Needs a management layer; test coverage per site</td></tr><tr><td>LTE-M or NB-IoT module</td><td>Built for small, infrequent messages and long battery life</td><td>Check network support per country and per carrier before committing</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>For low-data monitoring devices, LTE-M and NB-IoT are the usual cellular choices because they are designed for small messages and long battery life. Our comparison of <a href="/esim/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap/">NB-IoT, LTE-M and 5G RedCap</a> sets out the trade-offs, and the <a href="/esim/iot-sim-cards-guide/">IoT SIM guide</a> covers the SIM formats. Whichever you choose, confirm that the carriers you plan to use support that technology in each country where you will deploy.</p>
<h2 id="single-carrier-risks">Where single-carrier connectivity fails</h2>
<p>A device on one carrier shares that carrier&#8217;s fate. Three failures matter most for health devices.</p>
<h3 id="coverage-gaps">Coverage gaps</h3>
<p>No carrier covers every basement, farmhouse or interior corner. If the device cannot register, it cannot send, and a home test done in one place says little about another. Coverage should be checked at representative addresses, not assumed from a map.</p>
<h3 id="outages">Carrier outages</h3>
<p>Even large networks go down. The FCC&#8217;s report on the February 2024 AT&#038;T Mobility outage found that it lasted at least 12 hours, affected more than 125 million devices, blocked more than 92 million voice calls and prevented more than 25,000 calls to 911 call centers. The cause was an internal error: a misconfigured network element placed into production during a maintenance window. The lesson for device design is not about any one carrier. It is that a device with one SIM profile has no way to try another network when its own is unavailable. See the FCC&#8217;s <a href="https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-404150A1.pdf" rel="noopener">report and findings</a>.</p>
<h3 id="network-shutdowns">Network shutdowns</h3>
<p>Carriers retire old technology. In the US, AT&#038;T began phasing out 3G in February 2022, T-Mobile shut down its 3G UMTS network in July 2022 and Verizon completed its shutdown in December 2022. The FCC&#8217;s consumer guide notes that the phase-out is not limited to phones: certain medical devices, smartwatches, vehicle SOS services and home security systems may also rely on 3G. Devices built on a retired network need replacing or a hardware change. Programs that plan for the next shutdown choose modules that support current technologies, and use eSIM so a carrier profile can change without a truck roll. See the FCC&#8217;s <a href="https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/plan-ahead-phase-out-3g-cellular-networks-and-service" rel="noopener">3G phase-out guide</a>. Our piece on <a href="/telecom/single-carrier-iot-risks-2025/">single-carrier IoT risks</a> covers the wider pattern.</p>
<h2 id="multi-carrier-esim">How multi-carrier SIM and eSIM help</h2>
<p>A multi-carrier SIM holds access to several networks, and the device attaches to the best one available at its location. If that network degrades or fails, the device can move to another. With eSIM, the carrier profiles can also be added, swapped or retired remotely, which is how a fleet outlives a network shutdown without being recalled. The standards behind that remote management are explained in our guides to <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 and SGP.32</a> and to <a href="/esim/esim-orchestration-global-iot/">eSIM orchestration for global IoT</a>.</p>
<figure><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="https://media.spenza.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthcare-multi-network-failover-orange.webp" alt="A medical monitoring device in a home connects to two cell towers, with one tower marked as unavailable and the second carrying the signal" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Multi-network failover: when the first tower is down or out of reach, the device attaches to another instead of staying silent.</figcaption></figure>
<p>What this gives a health program:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Wider coverage per location.</strong> A pendant that finds no signal on one network may find another.</li>
<li><strong>Failover during outages.</strong> The device tries another network instead of waiting for the first to return. Ask how long switching takes and whether you can measure it.</li>
<li><strong>One device model for many regions.</strong> Fewer SKUs to stock and certify than a SIM per country.</li>
<li><strong>Lifecycle changes without swaps.</strong> Remote profile changes mean fewer shipments and fewer visits to patients.</li>
</ul>
<p>What it does not give: a guarantee of delivery. A device in a shielded room, with a flat battery, or in a region where no partner network reaches, still cannot connect. Treat multi-carrier connectivity as a way to remove a single point of failure, then keep monitoring for devices that go quiet.</p>
<h2 id="elder-care-medical-alerts">Elder care and medical alert systems</h2>
<p>Personal emergency response systems (PERS), medical alert pendants and watches have the same connectivity problem in a sharper form. The user may be alone, the device may be worn anywhere, and the only message is a button press. The CDC reports that over 14 million, or 1 in 4, older adults report falling every year, and that falls are the leading cause of injury for adults aged 65 and older, which is why these devices exist. See the CDC&#8217;s <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/index.html" rel="noopener">falls data</a>.</p>
<p>The requirements that differ from other RPM devices:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The device must work away from home.</strong> A pendant is useful on a walk or in a car park, so a home Wi-Fi hub is not enough. Use cellular, and test outdoors and at the edges of the area you serve.</li>
<li><strong>It must work on first power-up.</strong> Many users will not troubleshoot. A SIM that attaches wherever it is switched on lets one SKU ship nationwide.</li>
<li><strong>The monitoring center needs a signal when the device goes quiet.</strong> A device that has dropped offline looks the same as a flat battery or a coverage hole. Keep a heartbeat check-in and an escalation rule for a missed one. Fleet visibility by carrier and location helps separate a network problem from a hardware fault, so support does not replace devices that only needed a signal.</li>
<li><strong>Cost must stay predictable.</strong> These devices send little data, so per-line and per-megabyte charges that move when a device switches network create billing surprises. Pooled plans share allowance across the fleet; ask how roaming between partner networks is billed. Our <a href="/telecom/iot-connectivity-cost-guide-2025/">IoT connectivity cost guide</a> explains the pricing models.</li>
</ul>
<p>Remote monitoring for chronic conditions and PERS can share a platform, as long as each fleet has its own alert path. A missed blood-pressure reading and a missed fall alert call for very different responses, and your escalation rules should say so.</p>
<h2 id="security-compliance">Security and compliance questions to ask</h2>
<p>This section is a list of questions, not a statement of what the law requires of you. Whether a rule applies depends on who you are, what data moves and where. Have counsel review it.</p>
<h3 id="hipaa">US: HIPAA</h3>
<p>HIPAA applies to covered entities (such as providers and health plans) and their business associates. The US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) states in its cloud computing guidance that a cloud service provider that maintains electronic protected health information on behalf of a covered entity is a business associate even if it holds no decryption key, that a business associate agreement (BAA) is needed, and that encryption alone does not meet the Security Rule&#8217;s requirements for confidentiality, integrity and availability. HHS also says the covered entity and the provider should document in writing who handles each Security Rule requirement. See <a href="https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html" rel="noopener">HHS guidance on HIPAA and cloud computing</a>.</p>
<p>That guidance is about cloud providers. Whether a connectivity provider is a business associate depends on whether it stores or otherwise handles protected health information, which depends on the architecture. Ask the vendor directly, and ask counsel what your role requires.</p>
<h3 id="fda">US: FDA and device cybersecurity</h3>
<p>If you make a medical device, the FDA has issued guidance on cybersecurity in medical devices, covering design, labeling and premarket submission documentation, including section 524B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act for cyber devices. The FDA issued the current version in February 2026. See the FDA&#8217;s <a href="https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/cybersecurity-medical-devices-quality-management-system-considerations-and-content-premarket" rel="noopener">cybersecurity guidance page</a>. Your regulatory team decides whether your product is in scope and how the connection is described in a submission.</p>
<h3 id="eu">EU: MDR and GDPR</h3>
<p>In the EU, medical devices fall under the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR), and the European Commission publishes the framework on its <a href="https://health.ec.europa.eu/medical-devices-sector/new-regulations_en" rel="noopener">medical devices regulations page</a>. Personal data about health is also subject to the GDPR, which restricts processing of data concerning health and requires security measures appropriate to the risk. Member states can add conditions. Check the specific obligations for your product and markets with EU counsel.</p>
<h3 id="vendor-questions">Questions for any connectivity vendor</h3>
<ul>
<li>Can device traffic stay off the public internet through a private APN or VPN?</li>
<li>Can SIMs be locked to a specific device (IMEI lock) so a stolen SIM cannot be reused?</li>
<li>Who can see what in the portal, and are roles separated?</li>
<li>What activity is logged, for how long, and can you export it?</li>
<li>Where is data stored, and will the vendor sign a BAA or equivalent data processing terms if your data is regulated?</li>
<li>What happens to your devices and data if you end the contract?</li>
</ul>
<p>Get the answers in writing and attach them to your risk assessment. Marketing claims about compliance are not evidence.</p>
<h2 id="management-platform">Why fleet visibility matters</h2>
<p>A health program with thousands of devices needs to answer simple questions quickly. Is this device online? On which carrier? Is it using more data than it should? Did it check in today? A connectivity management platform (CMP) gives one place to see and act on those answers instead of a separate portal per carrier. Our <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">comparison of connectivity management platforms</a> lists the capabilities worth testing, and the piece on a <a href="/telecom/single-pane-of-glass-iot-connectivity-management/">single pane of glass</a> explains the monitoring side.</p>
<p>For health use, prioritize:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Per-device state and history</strong>: network, signal quality, last seen, and past failovers.</li>
<li><strong>Alerting on silence</strong>: a rule for devices that have not reported within their expected interval.</li>
<li><strong>Remote lifecycle control</strong>: activate, suspend and change profiles without a visit.</li>
<li><strong>API access</strong>: so connectivity events reach your own support and monitoring tools.</li>
<li><strong>Pooled pricing</strong>: one allowance across the fleet, with usage alerts.</li>
</ul>

For a health program the same checklist applies to us as to anyone: ask for a demonstration against your scenarios, ask for security and contract terms in writing, and run a pilot before volume orders. The full capability list is on the <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">platform comparison page</a>.</p>
<h2 id="rollout-checklist">A rollout checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Define the failure you can tolerate.</strong> How long can a reading be late? How long can an alert path be down? These numbers drive every other choice.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the connection model.</strong> Embedded cellular, hub, or both. Decide what the device does with readings while offline.</li>
<li><strong>Check radio technology per country.</strong> Confirm the module and the carriers support each other where you deploy.</li>
<li><strong>Test at real addresses.</strong> Include basements, rural homes and apartment buildings. Record signal and recovery time for each.</li>
<li><strong>Simulate loss of the primary network.</strong> Measure how long a device takes to reach another and whether the platform shows it.</li>
<li><strong>Define silent-device handling.</strong> Who is notified when a device has not checked in, and what do they do?</li>
<li><strong>Review security and contract terms.</strong> Private connectivity, access roles, logs, data location, BAA or equivalent, exit terms.</li>
<li><strong>Pilot, then scale.</strong> Run a small group for several weeks and review failures before ordering volume.</li>
</ol>
<p>For more on the options, see our guides to <a href="/iot/iot-connectivity-comparison/">comparing IoT connectivity options</a> and <a href="/iot/what-is-m2m-machine-to-machine-sims/">M2M SIMs</a>.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Healthcare IoT depends on a connection nobody sees until it fails. Treat connectivity as part of the product: choose a model that works without patient setup, avoid tying devices to one carrier or one network technology, keep fleet-wide visibility, and test where patients are. Handle compliance as a set of written questions to vendors and advisers, not as a feature claim. This article is general information, not legal or medical advice, and your counsel and regulatory team should review decisions that depend on the rules above.</p>

<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>What is IoT connectivity in healthcare?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>It is the network link that carries data between medical or health-monitoring devices and the systems that use that data: a clinic&#8217;s software, a monitoring center or a cloud platform. The link can be Wi-Fi, a cellular SIM in the device, or a cellular gateway that several devices share. The choice decides whether readings and alerts arrive when they are supposed to.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>How does remote patient monitoring connectivity work?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>A device at the patient&#8217;s home captures a reading and sends it, directly over a cellular SIM or through a hub on the patient&#8217;s Wi-Fi, to a cloud service. The care team&#8217;s software reads it from there. Each step can fail independently, so a good design assumes the first connection will sometimes be unavailable and defines what the device does next.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Why use a multi-carrier SIM for a medical alert or PERS device?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>A SIM tied to one carrier shares that carrier&#8217;s coverage gaps and outages. A multi-carrier SIM or eSIM can register on another network when the first is weak or down. It does not make an alert certain to get through, but it removes one common single point of failure, and it means one device model can be shipped to many regions.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Is a multi-carrier SIM enough to make a device HIPAA compliant?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>No. HIPAA applies to covered entities and their business associates and concerns how protected health information is handled, not only how it is transported. Connectivity is one control among many. Whether a vendor will sign a business associate agreement, and what it covers, is a contract question to settle with that vendor and your counsel.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>What should I ask a connectivity provider about security?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Ask whether device traffic can be kept off the public internet with a private APN or VPN, whether SIMs can be locked to a specific device, who can see what in the management portal, what logs are kept and for how long, and whether the provider will sign a business associate agreement if your data is regulated. Get the answers in writing.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>What happens to a device when a network is shut down?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>A device built for one network technology stops working when that network is retired. The 3G shutdowns in the US in 2022 showed this. Choose hardware with a module that supports current networks such as LTE-M or NB-IoT, and use eSIM so carrier profiles can be changed remotely instead of replacing units.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>Does the same platform work for remote monitoring and elder care alert devices?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Often yes. Both are low-data devices where a missed connection matters more than throughput, so the same needs apply: multi-network coverage, failover, fleet visibility and predictable pooled pricing. Check that the platform can group the two fleets separately, since their alert paths and escalation rules usually differ.</p></div></details>
<details name="mh-faq"><summary><h3>What is the best way to test connectivity before a rollout?</h3></summary><div class="mh-faq-answer"><p>Test in the places where users actually are, such as basements, rural addresses and apartment buildings. Switch off the primary network on a test device and measure how long recovery takes. Leave devices powered but idle for days to check-in behavior. Record results per location before ordering volume.</p></div></details>
