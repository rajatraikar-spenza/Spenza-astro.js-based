<div id="spenza-merged-article" class="mh-article">
<p>The IoT connectivity trends that matter in 2026 are remote SIM provisioning under GSMA SGP.32, integrated SIMs, multi-carrier connectivity managed as software, 5G RedCap, satellite (non-terrestrial network) fallback, the sunsets of 2G and 3G, and new security rules such as the EU Cyber Resilience Act. Most of them are real. Fewer are ready to buy for every device in every country, and the useful question is which stage each one has reached.</p>
<p>This guide sorts each trend into three stages: a published standard, a service you can order, and a deployment someone has accepted in the field. It uses dated primary sources and drops the forecasts it could not trace to one. The practical call for most fleets is to move now on carrier flexibility and 2G/3G exposure, pilot RedCap and satellite where the use case needs them, and watch integrated SIM until your module vendor ships it.</p>
<p><em>Last verified 2 November 2026.</em></p>
<h2 id="where-iot-connectivity-stands">Where IoT connectivity stands in 2026</h2>
<p><a href="https://iot-analytics.com/number-connected-iot-devices/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">IoT Analytics</a> counted 18.5 billion connected IoT devices at the end of 2024 and expects 21.1 billion at the end of 2025, rising to 39 billion by 2030. Cellular is 22% of that count.</p>
<p>The cellular share is tracked separately. The <a href="https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/mobility-report/dataforecasts/iot-connections-outlook" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Ericsson Mobility Report (June 2026)</a> puts total cellular IoT connections at around 4.5 billion at the end of 2025 and forecasts about 7.8 billion by the end of 2031, a 10% compound annual growth rate. About 2.6 billion of the 2025 total, roughly 60%, were broadband and critical IoT on 4G and 5G, and that segment is expected to keep the largest share through 2031. The remainder is mostly massive IoT on NB-IoT and LTE-M.</p>
<p>The two sources count differently, so do not add them or compare them. Both point the same way: a larger installed base, a shift toward 4G and 5G, and devices that stay in the field for years and so must survive network, supplier and regulation changes.</p>
<h2 id="trend-readiness">How to read a trend: standard, offer, deployment</h2>
<p>Most trend articles blur three different claims. A <strong>published standard</strong> defines how something should work. A <strong>commercial offer</strong> is something a supplier will sell you, in named regions and for named devices. An <strong>accepted deployment</strong> is a result a customer has recorded in the field. Evidence at one stage does not establish the next, and a roadmap built on the wrong stage is how projects slip.</p>
<p>Where a supplier is named below, it is an example of an offer that exists, not a ranking.</p>

<h2 id="esim-sgp32">Trend 1: IoT eSIM and SGP.32 move from standard to product</h2>
<p>Remote SIM provisioning for IoT used to mean choosing between the consumer architecture, which expects a person to scan a code, and the older M2M architecture, which was server-driven but hard to make interoperable. The <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/SGP.32-v1.2.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">GSMA SGP.32 version 1.2</a> (published June 2024) defines an architecture for IoT with two components: an eSIM IoT Manager (eIM) on the management side and an IoT Profile Assistant (IPA) on the device or eUICC side. The device does not need a screen or a user to change its operator profile.</p>
<p>Two dated product anchors show the standard becoming an offer. Soracom announced the commercial release of its SGP.32-compatible IoT eSIMs on 8 July 2026, after a pre-order period; the company ties full multi-operator capability to partner integrations being completed. Expect staggered availability: the specification exists everywhere, but a working combination of module, eUICC, eIM and operator profile exists in fewer places.</p>
<p>On adoption, Counterpoint Research projected in February 2024 that IoT connections on eSIM would grow about 43% a year to reach 2.2 billion by 2030, from under 200 million at the end of 2023. That is a forecast from a single analyst firm made before the standard was commercial, so treat it as an indication of direction and not a planning number.</p>
<div class="mh-callout"><p><strong>What this means for buyers:</strong> an &#8220;eSIM&#8221; label does not tell you which architecture a device implements. Put the SGP version, the eIM provider and the profile sources in the purchase specification, and ask who holds the eIM role if you change platform. The standard guide to the choice is in <a href="/esim/sgp-22-vs-sgp-32-esim-standards-guide/">SGP.22 versus SGP.32</a>.</p></div>
<p>Remote provisioning does not remove the network contract. A profile is a subscription that still needs a carrier agreement and a bootstrap connection to download from. Decide what the device does if a download fails halfway, and test it before shipping.</p>
<h2 id="isim">Trend 2: integrated SIM is real, but earlier than eSIM</h2>
<p>An integrated SIM (iSIM) moves the secure SIM function inside the main chip instead of a separate eUICC component. That can save board space and power, which matters for small, battery-powered devices. Thales and Qualcomm announced the first GSMA-certified iSIM on a commercially deployable Snapdragon 8 Gen 2 platform on 27 February 2023.</p>
<p>That is a named implementation on a named chip, not proof that every IoT module offers iSIM, nor that your provisioning platform supports it. The sensible rule is simple: if a device already supports eSIM, deploy it and benefit from remote provisioning now. Treat iSIM as a design option to evaluate when your module vendor can show certified, available parts, and choose it only if the size or power gain changes the product.</p>
<h2 id="multi-carrier">Trend 3: connectivity becomes a software-controlled, multi-carrier layer</h2>
<p>The most durable trend is not a radio or a chip. It is the shift from picking one carrier at shipment to treating connectivity as something you configure over the life of the device. Remote profiles make the switching possible, and a management layer makes it practical: one place to see devices, usage, status and cost across carriers and countries, with rules that act on them.</p>
<p>The reasoning is operational. A single carrier is one set of coverage maps, one price book and one outage domain. Devices that cross borders face roaming terms that differ by country, and long-lived devices outlast the commercial terms they were bought under. A multi-carrier or carrier-agnostic model lets you change the supplier of a market without touching the hardware. The risks of the alternative are covered in <a href="/telecom/single-carrier-iot-risks-2025/">single-carrier IoT risks</a>, and the management side in <a href="/telecom/single-pane-of-glass-iot-connectivity-management/">single-pane-of-glass connectivity management</a>.</p>
<p>Be careful with savings claims: no primary source checked for this guide supports a general percentage. Compare current spend per active device-month against a quote on a defined scope, including integration, support and exit costs. The <a href="/telecom/iot-connectivity-cost-guide-2025/">IoT connectivity cost guide</a> lists the inputs.</p>
<p>Automation sits on top of this layer. Rules that move a device to another profile after a coverage drop, or cap spend per device, do not need machine learning. AI can help triage exceptions and propose changes, but a proposal is not an authorization: define which actions may run without a human, set limits, and keep an auditable change record. Start with one bounded workflow, such as flagging devices on paid plans with no usage.</p>
<h2 id="redcap-lpwa">Trend 4: RedCap fills the middle, LPWA keeps the low end</h2>
<p>5G Reduced Capability (RedCap) was introduced in 3GPP Release 17 to bring 5G to mid-range devices, between low-power wide-area (LPWA) technologies and full 5G broadband. <a href="https://www.3gpp.org/technologies/redcap" rel="noopener" target="_blank">3GPP</a> lists wearables for medical, business and personal monitoring and industrial sensors on campuses and factories among its target uses, and Release 18 added enhanced RedCap work and more bands.</p>
<p>On the market side, the Ericsson Mobility Report states that 14 service providers had commercially launched 5G RedCap and that 42 service providers in 27 countries were investing in it. It also says enhanced RedCap, with peak rates of 10 Mbps, enters lab trials in 2026 with full commercial potential expected from 2028. So RedCap is an offer in some networks and still early in others, and enhanced RedCap is a standard moving toward product.</p>
<p>RedCap does not retire NB-IoT or LTE-M. Meters, trackers and sensors that send small payloads infrequently and need long battery life are still served by LPWA at lower module cost. RedCap makes sense when you need more throughput or lower latency than LPWA, or a 5G standalone feature, and cannot justify full 5G module cost and power. Match the category to the payload, mobility and battery requirement using the <a href="/esim/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap/">NB-IoT, LTE-M and RedCap comparison</a>, and check that your target markets actually have a RedCap-enabled network before you design for it.</p>
<h2 id="satellite-ntn">Trend 5: satellite joins cellular through NTN</h2>
<p>Satellite IoT is no longer only a separate proprietary network. According to <a href="https://www.3gpp.org/technologies/ntn-overview" rel="noopener" target="_blank">3GPP</a>, Release 17 was the first with normative requirements for non-terrestrial networks (NTN), covering NR-NTN and IoT-NTN for NB-IoT and eMTC (LTE-M); RAN4 requirements followed in Release 18. Release 18 added further bands, and Release 19 continues the work, including store-and-forward operation and RedCap devices over NR-NTN. The direction is one SIM identity and one device class that can use towers where they exist and satellites where they do not.</p>
<p>The commercial side is narrower than the standard. Soracom announced general availability of its Skylo-based satellite integration on 2 October 2025, for customers in North America, Europe, Latin America and Oceania, with satellite coverage activated from the same platform as cellular. That supports the existence of one offer. It does not prove coverage in every building, compatibility with arbitrary hardware, or uninterrupted sessions during a network change.</p>
<p>A satellite path is an addition, not a replacement. Use it for the traffic that has to get out of a dead zone, such as alarms, positions and a daily summary, and check message size, latency and airtime pricing with the provider. Define which payloads may use it, cap the volume, and test two moments explicitly: losing terrestrial service, and getting back onto it. An eSIM profile cannot add satellite capability to a modem that lacks the radio, bands and firmware; confirm the exact module.</p>

<h2 id="sunsets">Trend 6: 2G and 3G sunsets keep forcing migration</h2>
<p>This one is not a forecast; it is already happening. The Ericsson Mobility Report states that by the end of 2025, 80 service providers had fully shut down 3G and 46 had switched off 2G. It expects 3G phase-out to move faster than 2G in many markets because the spectrum strengthens 4G and 5G, while 2G stays longer for voice and legacy IoT.</p>
<p>A concrete national example is the UK. <a href="https://ofcom.org.uk/phones-and-broadband/coverage-and-speeds/advice-for-iot-and-third-party-device-suppliers" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Ofcom</a> states that all four UK operators have completed their 3G switch-offs, so 3G-only SIMs no longer work. Operators have published 2G dates that they may change: EE from May 2029, O2 starting summer 2029, and Vodafone during 2030. O2 also stopped inbound 2G roaming on 1 October 2025, which affects devices on overseas roaming SIMs. Ofcom places responsibility on the service provider: if your service relies on 2G, you must make sure it keeps working, and devices that only support 2G need to move to at least 4G.</p>
<p>Dates differ by country, so build your own inventory: for every deployed device, record the module, supported technologies, the network it attaches to and the countries it operates in, then match that to published operator dates. At greatest risk are 2G-only trackers, older meters, alarms and payment terminals, and units that fall back to 2G or 3G when LTE is weak. Replacing them takes sourcing and field time, so start early.</p>
<p>Remote profile management helps with the network side of a sunset, because a device can move to a new carrier profile without a site visit. It cannot make a 2G-only radio speak LTE.</p>

<h2 id="security-regulation">Trend 7: security rules reach connected products</h2>
<p>Regulation is turning security from a best practice into an obligation for products sold in some markets. The <a href="https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/cyber-resilience-act" rel="noopener" target="_blank">European Commission</a> gives the Cyber Resilience Act timeline: it entered into force on 10 December 2024, reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026, and the main obligations from 11 December 2027. From September 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities, and they must handle security flaws across the product life.</p>
<p>For connectivity, the consequences are practical. Make sure you can identify which devices run which firmware, patch them over the air, and revoke or rotate credentials. A SIM acts as a secure element, and remote provisioning lets you replace a compromised subscription profile without a visit. None of that makes a product compliant by itself: the Act covers the whole product, and whether your device is in scope is a question for your own legal and security review. Do not accept a supplier&rsquo;s compliance slogan in place of an assessment against the Commission&rsquo;s guidance.</p>
<h2 id="iot-mvno">Trend 8: IoT-focused operators and connectivity-as-a-service</h2>
<p>Enterprises increasingly buy connectivity from specialist providers that run on top of mobile network operators rather than from the operator directly, and operators and MVNOs are building IoT-specific plans, billing and APIs. The attraction is plan structure that fits devices: pooled data, per-device limits, usage alerts and one invoice instead of several. The trade-off is that you add a supplier, and you should confirm what the provider controls and what it resells. The comparison is covered in <a href="/mvno/iot-mvnos-vs-traditional/">IoT MVNOs versus traditional operators</a>, and the platform side in <a href="/esim/leading-iot-connectivity-management-platforms/">IoT connectivity management platforms</a>.</p>
<p>For operators and MVNOs moving into IoT, the <a href="/mvno/mno-mvno-mvne-mvna-roles/">MVNO, MVNE and MVNA roles</a> guide explains who provides network access, billing and provisioning.</p>
<p>Spenza supplies an IoT connectivity management and MVNE platform. The <a href="/spenza-product/butlr-iot-spenza-casestudy/">Butlr case study</a> describes tailored plans, centralized operations and unified billing for sensors in the US, UK and France. It shows plan fit and administration, not a benchmark for any technology above.</p>

<h2 id="evaluate-a-trend">How to evaluate a trend for your own fleet</h2>
<p>Start with the constraint you have, not the trend. If SIM swaps cost truck rolls, remote provisioning is relevant. If devices go dark outside cities, look at satellite. If an operator has announced a 2G date, the hardware plan comes first. Anything else belongs on a watch list.</p>
<ol><li><strong>Name the problem and baseline.</strong> Current cost per active device-month, outage hours, truck rolls and replacement cycles.</li><li><strong>Confirm the exact configuration.</strong> Module, eUICC, firmware, profile source, markets and operator list, written down with dates.</li><li><strong>Pilot with acceptance criteria.</strong> Delivery success, power, cost and recovery after failure. Include a failed profile download and a lost network.</li><li><strong>Check ownership.</strong> Who holds profiles, who can switch supplier, and what happens when the contract ends.</li><li><strong>Decide with a record.</strong> Keep the dated specification, offer and tested configuration.</li></ol>
<p>The <a href="/iot/iot-connectivity-comparison/">IoT connectivity comparison</a> helps shortlist technologies for the actual workload.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>The IoT connectivity trends of 2026 sort cleanly. Carrier flexibility through eSIM and a management layer is ready for most new fleets. RedCap and satellite are real but limited, so pilot them where the use case demands. iSIM is a design option, not a plan. The 2G and 3G sunsets and the Cyber Resilience Act are dated obligations that decide your schedule whether or not you like the technology.</p>
<p>Keep a dated record of what you verified, because this field changes quickly. The choices that keep a device manageable for a decade are mostly about ownership, exit and recovery, not the newest radio.</p>
