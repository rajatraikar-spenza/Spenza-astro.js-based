<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What are IoT Sensors? </strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">IoT sensors are hardware devices that detect physical conditions like temperature, motion, or pressure and transmit that data over a network to software platforms for monitoring and automation. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The global IoT sensor market was valued at $23.9 Billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $381.6 Billion by 2034, growing at a 36.1% CAGR (<a href="https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/iot-sensors-market" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">GMI, 2025</a>). With over 626.7 million sensor units deployed in 2024 alone, businesses are realizing that picking the right sensor is only half the job. The other half is connecting it correctly. A vibration sensor in a factory may need LTE-M for reliable mobility and firmware updates, while a soil sensor in a remote field may work best on low-power NB-IoT. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In this blog, you will find the sensor types explained, the connectivity matched to each one, the top OEM vendors compared, and a simple framework for making sourcing decisions in 2026.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How IoT Sensors Work in Connected Devices </strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At a basic level, IoT sensors detect physical changes in the environment and convert them into digital signals. A sensor does not just measure something and send it to the cloud.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A simple architecture usually looks like this:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Sensor element</strong> detects a physical change → <strong>Signal conditioning</strong> cleans up the raw signal → <strong>Microcontroller (MCU)</strong> processes it → <strong>Connectivity module</strong> transmits the data → <strong>Cloud or edge platform</strong> stores and analyzes it.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="777" height="426" class="wp-image-17608" style="width: 777px;" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8-1024x562.png" alt="IoT Sensor Architecture Diagram" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8-1024x562.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8-300x165.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8-768x421.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8-1536x843.png 1536w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-8.png 1693w" sizes="(max-width: 777px) 100vw, 777px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Two terms worth knowing here:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>MEMS sensors (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems):</strong> Tiny, silicon-fabricated sensors. They dominate the market because they are cheap, accurate, and power-efficient. Your phone&#8217;s accelerometer is a MEMS sensor.</li>



<li><strong>Traditional sensors:</strong> Larger, older-technology sensors, often used in heavy industrial settings where extreme durability matters more than size.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It’s also important to distinguish between sensors and actuators: </p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Sensors:</strong> Detect and report. (Collects Data)</li>



<li><strong>Actuators:</strong> Respond and act. (Performs action)</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A temperature sensor tells you it is too hot. An actuator turns the fan on. Both are often found in the same IoT device, but they are different components.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Most Common Types of IoT Sensors Explained</strong></h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="666" height="444" class="wp-image-17610" style="width: 666px;" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sensor-Categoization-Diagram-1024x683.png" alt="IoT sensor categories diagram, including environmental, motion, optical, biomedical, and industrial sensors 
" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sensor-Categoization-Diagram-1024x683.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sensor-Categoization-Diagram-300x200.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sensor-Categoization-Diagram-768x512.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sensor-Categoization-Diagram.png 1264w" sizes="(max-width: 666px) 100vw, 666px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Rather than a flat list, here are the major sensor types grouped by what they actually do. This makes it easier to match a sensor to your use case.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. Motion and Position</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Accelerometers:</strong> Detect movement, vibration, and shock. Used in wearables, vehicle tracking, and predictive maintenance.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Gyroscopes:</strong> Measure orientation and rotation. Used in drones, robotics, and navigation.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>IMUs (Inertial Measurement Units):</strong> Combine accelerometer and gyroscope. Used in asset tracking and autonomous vehicles.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>GPS/GNSS sensors:</strong> Track location outdoors. Used in logistics, fleet management, and agriculture.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2. Environmental</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Temperature sensors:</strong> The most widely deployed sensor type globally. Used in cold chain, HVAC, and industrial monitoring.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Humidity sensors:</strong> Often paired with temperature. Used in agriculture, pharmaceuticals, and data centers.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Pressure sensors:</strong> Measure gas or liquid pressure. Used in weather stations, water leak detection, and industrial pipelines.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Gas and air quality sensors:</strong> Detect CO2, VOCs, and particulate matter. Used in smart buildings for industrial safety.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>3. Optical and Imaging</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Image sensors:</strong> Capture visual data. Used in security cameras and traffic monitoring.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Ambient light sensors:</strong> Adjust display brightness or trigger lighting. Used in smart streetlights and consumer electronics.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Infrared (IR) sensors:</strong> Detect heat signatures. Used in occupancy detection, thermal imaging.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>LiDAR:</strong> Maps 3D environments using laser pulses. Used in autonomous vehicles and robotics.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>4. Acoustic</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Microphones:</strong> Capture sound for voice commands, noise monitoring.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Ultrasonic sensors:</strong> Measure distance using sound waves. Used in parking sensors and level detection.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5. Proximity and Presence</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>PIR sensors:</strong> Detect human movement via infrared. Used in security and smart lighting.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Capacitive sensors:</strong> Detect touch or proximity. Used in touchscreens and smart appliances.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>6. Vibration and Strain</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Vibration sensors:</strong> Monitor rotating machinery and structural movement. Used in predictive maintenance, industrial equipment monitoring, and bridge health assessment.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Strain sensors:</strong> Measure mechanical deformation and stress on surfaces. Used in civil infrastructure, aerospace, and heavy manufacturing.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>7. Chemical and Water Quality</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Chemical sensors:</strong> Detect specific gases or compounds in the surrounding environment. Used in industrial leak detection, food safety, and laboratory automation.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Water quality sensors:</strong> Measure pH, turbidity, and dissolved oxygen levels in water bodies. Used in environmental monitoring, aquaculture, and municipal water treatment.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>8. Biomedical</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Heart rate and SpO2 sensors:</strong> Monitor cardiovascular activity and blood oxygen saturation continuously. Used in fitness wearables, hospital patient monitoring, and remote care devices.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Glucose sensors:</strong> Track blood sugar levels in real time. Used in diabetes management devices and clinical trials.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Electrodermal and temperature sensors:</strong> Measure skin conductance and body temperature for stress and fever detection. Used in mental health monitoring and emergency response wearables.</li>
</ul>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Worth Noting</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">You do not always need a <span style="font-weight: bold;">single sensor</span>. Many modern <span style="font-weight: bold;">IoT devices</span> rely on <span style="font-weight: bold;">sensor fusion</span>, combining data from multiple sensors to create a more accurate understanding of the environment. For example, a <span style="font-weight: bold;">fitness tracker</span> may combine an <span style="font-weight: bold;">accelerometer</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">heart rate sensor</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold;">temperature sensor</span> simultaneously.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Three New IoT Sensor Categories Defining 2026</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These categories barely existed three years ago. They matter now.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. AI-on-edge sensors</strong> </h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Traditional sensors simply collect data. AI-on-edge sensors can process data locally. They run machine learning models directly on the sensor chip, without sending data to the cloud first. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Bosch BHI360 and Nordic nRF54LM20B with Axon NPU are real examples. The benefit is faster anomaly detection, lower data transmission costs, and better privacy. Think of it like having a mini analyst sitting inside the sensor itself.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2. Satellite-NTN sensors</strong> </h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It connects directly to non-terrestrial networks (satellites), not cellular towers. The Nordic nRF92 series supports this via 3GPP Release 17/18 standards. This makes remote deployments far more practical, like deep-sea monitoring, polar research stations, or offshore oil rigs where no cellular signal exists.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>3.</strong> <strong>Vibration sensors for Predictive Maintenance</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It has been around for years, but they are now being paired with cellular connectivity and AI to predict equipment failure before it happens. Studies estimate predictive maintenance enabled by IoT can reduce machine downtime by 30-50% and extend machine life by 20-40%. (<a href="https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/industry-40-reimagining-manufacturing-operations-after-covid-19" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">McKinsey Global Institute, 2022</a>).</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How to Choose an IoT Sensor: A Framework by Use Case</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here is a simple way to think about sensor selection. Start with the use case, not the sensor.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>1. Smart agriculture:</strong> Soil moisture and temperature sensors transmitting via NB-IoT. Low power is critical. Sensors may run on a battery for years in fields without power access.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>2. Industrial predictive maintenance:</strong> Vibration and temperature sensors on factory equipment, transmitting via LTE-M. Firmware updates (FOTA) need to be possible remotely. <a href="https://spenza.com/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Spenza&#8217;s IoT connectivity management platform</a> helps manage these deployments at scale across multiple sites and carriers.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>3. Cold chain logistics:</strong> Temperature and humidity sensors in shipping containers, paired with NB-IoT or LTE-M depending on mobility. Multi-carrier eSIM matters here because containers cross borders.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>4. Connected healthcare wearables:</strong> Biomedical sensors (heart rate, SpO2) transmitting via BLE to a smartphone gateway, which then uses LTE-M to reach the cloud. Power budget is very tight.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>5. Smart city infrastructure:</strong> Air quality and parking sensors using NB-IoT. High device density, very low data per device, and long deployment lifetimes.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The easiest way to avoid overengineering is to ask: “<strong>How much data actually needs to move, and how often?</strong>”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That single question often determines the ideal connectivity model.</p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Sourcing Reminder</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">Always confirm <span style="font-weight: bold;">3GPP release support</span> on the <span style="font-weight: bold;">cellular module</span> paired with your sensor stack. Also verify <span style="font-weight: bold;">n-band compatibility</span> for the specific <span style="font-weight: bold;">deployment region</span> before finalizing hardware selections. A module that works in one market may have <span style="font-weight: bold;">limited carrier compatibility</span> or reduced performance in another.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Sensor and eSIM Lifecycle: Why It Matters for Scale</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you deploy one sensor, connectivity management is easy. When you deploy 10,000 sensors across 15 countries and need to physically replace SIM cards whenever carrier agreements change, it becomes a major operational problem. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is where <strong>eSIM (embedded SIM)</strong> becomes operationally important. Instead of physically inserting a SIM card into each device at deployment, eSIM allows you to remotely provision and switch carriers over the air. The SGP.32 standard, finalised by the <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/esim/gsma_resources/sgp-32-v1-1/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">GSMA</a> in late 2025, is the IoT-specific eSIM specification designed to handle exactly this at scale.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Common Mistakes in IoT Sensor Sourcing</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These are the errors that cause expensive redesigns.</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Choosing the sensor before choosing the connectivity technology</li>



<li>Ignoring 3GPP release support on the cellular module</li>



<li>Overlooking n-band coverage in the target deployment region</li>



<li>Not planning for FOTA (firmware-over-the-air) bandwidth requirements</li>



<li>Locking into a single carrier without a fallback option</li>



<li>Ignoring the 2G and 3G sunset timelines, which affect legacy module choices</li>
</ul>
