<h2 id="what-is-io-t" class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What is IoT?</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To understand what an IoT device is, first we need to understand IoT.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>The Internet of Things (IoT)</strong>&nbsp;is a term that covers “<em>the network of physical objects that contain embedded technology to communicate and sense or interact with their internal states or the external environment,</em>” according to the Global System for Mobile Communications Association (GSMA).</p>



<h2 id="what-are-io-t-devices" class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What are IoT Devices?</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">IoT devices are specialized hardware like sensors, actuators, and appliances that connect wirelessly to networks to collect and exchange data. They perform specific tasks and are used across various fields, including medical, industrial, and environmental sectors, enhancing functionality through interconnectedness and real-time data communication.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But let’s deep dive into this term:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP), which develops and maintains technical specifications for mobile telecommunications, breaks up IoT devices into two categories;&nbsp;<strong>User Equipment (UE) and Infrastructure</strong>.</p>



<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>User Equipment: Devices that connect to networks (including mobile phones, IoT devices, and computers).</li>



<li>Infrastructure: The equipment UE connects to for telecommunication services and includes the access network and core network.</li>
</ol>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It’s interesting here that 3GPP keeps mobile phones and computers separate to IoT devices. So with the information we have at hand, how shall we define IoT devices?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A good definition for IoT devices is&nbsp;<em>“<strong>equipment that contains embedded technology to sense or interact with their environment and communicate with the internet.</strong>”</em></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Smart home voice assistants like Google Home and Amazon Alexa are two of the most known IoT devices. But the number of generic trackers and environmental monitors deployed far outstrips the number of home assistants globally.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You could argue that smartphones and computers are IoT devices; they can sense the physical world and communicate data on it to the cloud. You can certainly use them as expensive IoT devices, but you usually don’t say something is part of IoT when it requires human interaction or control.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph" id="examples-of-io-t-devices"><strong>Examples of IoT devices</strong>: Sensors, Security devices, Point of Sale (PoS) devices, Medical devices, GPS trackers</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How Many IoT Devices Exist in 2026?</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The number of IoT-connected devices, according to <a href="https://iot-analytics.com/product/state-of-enterprise-iot-2026-connected-operations-ai-era/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">IoT Analytics</a>, reached 21.1 billion globally in 2025, representing 14 percent year-over-year growth. The market is forecast to grow to 39 billion devices by 2030 and exceed 50 billion by 2035. Wi-Fi accounts for 32 percent of all connections, Bluetooth for 24 percent, and cellular IoT for 22 percent. In addition, cellular IoT chipset revenue is forecast to triple, reaching 14.08 billion dollars by 2030.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How Internet of Things (IoT) Devices Work</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every IoT device combines five elements: a sensor or actuator that interacts with the physical world, a microcontroller (MCU) that processes inputs, a connectivity module (cellular, Wi-Fi, BLE, or LPWAN), a power source (battery, harvested, or wired), and firmware that manages behavior over the air. The connectivity choice is the most consequential design decision because it locks in cost, coverage, and lifecycle for the next 5 to 15 years.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. For Consumer Brands: Devices That Carry Your Logo</strong></h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-3-1024x683.png" alt="For Consumer Brands: Devices That Carry Your Logo" class="wp-image-17591" style="width:555px" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-3-1024x683.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-3-300x200.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-3-768x512.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/image-3.png 1536w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consumer brand IoT devices are connected products sold directly to end users under your own brand. These include wearables, smartwatches, kids GPS trackers, branded eSIM phone services, and smart home hubs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The preferred connectivity stack is <strong>LTE-M combined with multi-carrier eSIM</strong>, supported by a compliance baseline that typically includes <strong>FCC, CE, and FCC Robocall Mitigation Database</strong> requirements.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">LTE-M is the primary connectivity choice for consumer IoT devices because it delivers a strong balance of performance and power efficiency. It provides enough bandwidth for app syncing and firmware-over-the-air (FOTA) updates while maintaining low power consumption that enables multi-day battery life. It is also the only LPWAN option that reliably supports mobility and seamless handovers, which is critical for devices that move with users.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In contrast, NB-IoT is generally not suitable for consumer use cases because it lacks mobility support, voice capabilities, and efficient firmware update handling.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For global product lines, multi-carrier eSIM has become a core requirement. <strong>SGP.22</strong> defines current consumer eSIM implementations, while <strong>SGP.32</strong> represents the next generation of eSIM provisioning and orchestration that product teams should design for going forward.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2026 New Device Categories: AI, Satellite, RedCap, and SGP.32</strong></h2>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. AI-on-device IoT hardware</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">AI-on-device hardware runs ML inference locally instead of streaming raw sensor data to the cloud. This cuts bandwidth, improves latency, and protects privacy. Reference parts include the Bosch BHI360, Nordic nRF54LM20B with Axon NPU, and TDK i3 Micro Module. Specify when sub-100ms response is required or raw data cannot legally leave the device.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2. Satellite NTN-capable IoT devices</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Satellite NTN, standardized in 3GPP Releases 17 and 18, makes satellite IoT mainstream. The Nordic nRF92 supports cellular plus satellite fallback in one chip. Use cases include maritime tracking, agricultural sensors, pipeline monitoring, and any deployment crossing cellular dead zones.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>3. 5G RedCap IoT devices</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G RedCap, introduced in 3GPP Release 17, is the purpose-built 5G standard for mid-tier IoT. It delivers high speed and low latency at half the cost and power of full 5G. RedCap replaces LTE Cat-4 in cameras, wearables, video telematics, and FWA CPE. Module prices are dropping fast.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>4. SGP.32-ready eSIM IoT devices</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SGP.32 is the GSMA eSIM standard built for IoT. Released in May 2023, it introduces eIM (eSIM IoT Remote Manager) for fleet-grade remote profile management on headless devices. SGP.32-ready devices switch operator profiles automatically based on coverage, cost, or policy, with no truck rolls.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How to Choose an IoT Device: The 7-Step Framework</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">1. &nbsp; Define use case and data volume. Tiny packets fit NB-IoT or LoRaWAN. Medium with mobility fits LTE-M. Rich data fits Cat-1, Cat-4, or 5G RedCap. Continuous streaming requires 5G NR or Wi-Fi 6.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">2. &nbsp; Choose connectivity. NB-IoT for stationary low-data. LTE-M for mobile low-latency. 5G RedCap for mid-tier. Wi-Fi 6 for indoor high-throughput. Multi-RAT for global SKUs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">3. &nbsp; Pick the power profile. 10+ year battery: NB-IoT or LoRaWAN with PSM and eDRX. 3 to 5 years with mobility: LTE-M. Wired or harvested: optimize for performance.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">4. &nbsp; Decide module vs full device. Certified modules cut certification by 6 to 12 months. Modules win for low volume or fast time-to-market. Chipset-down with iSIM wins for high-volume consumer.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5. &nbsp; Plan eSIM strategy. SGP.22 for consumer with UI today. SGP.32 for IoT and headless devices shipping in 2026 and beyond. iSIM for lowest-BOM designs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">6. &nbsp; Validate compliance. FCC for US, CE and RED for EU, RMD for voice, EU Cyber Resilience Act mandatory in 2027.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">7. &nbsp; Model 3-year TCO. Hardware is one line. SIM access fees, data plans, platform fees, integration, and support fill the rest. Multi-carrier with pooled data routinely cuts spend by 20 to 30 percent.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Common IoT Device Sourcing Pitfalls</strong></h3>



<ul class="wp-block-list">

<li>Choosing the device before choosing connectivity. Connectivity is harder to swap and locks the device into a coverage and cost envelope.</li>




<li>Ignoring the 2G and 3G sunset. Devices relying on 2G or 3G fallback are effectively decommissioned over time.</li>




<li>Skipping eSIM and SGP.32 future-proofing. A 10-year device without eUICC creates long-term dependency and truck-roll costs.</li>




<li>Undersizing FOTA bandwidth budgets. Firmware updates are ongoing—plan for continuous data usage.</li>




<li>Single-carrier lock-in. One outage or regulatory change can disrupt the entire deployment.</li>




<li>Ignoring CRA and PSTI compliance. Non-compliant devices risk being removed from the market.</li>




<li>Treating connectivity cost as fixed. It is often the most variable component of IoT TCO and one of the easiest to optimize.</li>

</ul>
