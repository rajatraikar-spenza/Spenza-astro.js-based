<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>mmWave vs Sub-6 vs Low-Band 5G: Three Different Networks Sharing One Name</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The single biggest misconception about 5G coverage is that 5G is one network. It is three, and the difference between them is the difference between 1 Gbps and 50 Mbps on the same phone.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="683" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_49_29-AM-1024x683.png" alt="mmWave vs Sub-6 vs Low-Band 5G" class="wp-image-18420" style="width:666px" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_49_29-AM-1024x683.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_49_29-AM-300x200.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_49_29-AM-768x512.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_49_29-AM.png 1536w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>mmWave 5G (24 GHz and above)</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Delivers peak speeds above 1 Gbps and latency below 15 ms. It is the speed your carrier advertises. Its weakness is range and penetration. A single mmWave cell covers about 1,000 feet outdoors and struggles to pass through walls, foliage, or heavy rain. mmWave 5G is concentrated in stadiums, airports, downtown cores, and select dense neighborhoods.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Sub-6 5G (C-band 3.5 to 4 GHz, plus 2.5 GHz)</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Delivers the bulk of real-world 5G traffic in 2026. Real-world speeds range from 150 to 500 Mbps depending on cell load and distance. Range is similar to 4G LTE. This is what most people experience when their phone shows the 5G icon.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Low-band 5G (600 to 900 MHz)</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Provides 5G coverage across entire states and rural areas. It penetrates buildings well and reaches across large distances. The catch: peak speeds are typically 50 to 200 Mbps, which is comparable to a good 4G LTE signal. The 5G icon stays lit, but the speed is not very different from what you had before.</p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Watch Out</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">If your phone displays a <span style="font-weight: bold;">5G icon</span> while connected only to <span style="font-weight: bold;">low-band spectrum</span>, the actual performance may feel very similar to <span style="font-weight: bold;">4G LTE</span>. Carriers often keep the <span style="font-weight: bold;">5G indicator</span> visible whenever technically possible, even when speeds are modest. To verify real-world performance, run a test using <span style="font-weight: bold;">Ookla Speedtest</span> or <span style="font-weight: bold;">Opensignal</span>. The network icon is ultimately a <span style="font-weight: bold;">marketing label</span>, not a guaranteed measure of speed or latency.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5G SA vs NSA: The Performance Gap Carriers Rarely Mention</strong></h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="576" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM-1024x576.png" alt="5G SA vs NSA architecture diagram" class="wp-image-18424" style="width:666px" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM-1024x576.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM-300x169.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM-768x432.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM-1536x864.png 1536w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChatGPT-Image-May-29-2026-10_59_18-AM.png 1672w" sizes="(max-width: 1024px) 100vw, 1024px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most 5G in the United States still runs on Non-Standalone Architecture (NSA), where the 5G radio rides on top of a 4G LTE control plane. NSA was the quickest path to 5G branding for carriers, but it caps the latency floor at around 30 ms because every connection setup still touches 4G core systems.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G Standalone (SA) uses an end-to-end 5G core network. It unlocks:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>20 to 30 percent lower latency compared to NSA, according to Opensignal SA market analysis.</li>



<li>Network slicing: dedicated virtual networks per use case (priority lanes for IoT, gaming, public safety).</li>



<li>Voice over New Radio (VoNR) for HD voice without falling back to LTE.</li>



<li>True service-level differentiation that carriers can sell as separate enterprise products.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">T-Mobile led US carriers to 5G SA in 2020. AT&amp;T completed its nationwide 5G SA deployment in 2025, validated in the <a href="https://insights.opensignal.com/reports/2026/01/usa/mobile-network-experience" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Opensignal January 2026 report</a>. Verizon has launched 5G SA in select markets but is still expanding.</p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Why SA Matters for IoT Buyers</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;"><span style="font-weight: bold;">Network slicing</span> only functions on <span style="font-weight: bold;">5G Standalone (SA) cores</span>. This enables organizations to deploy <span style="font-weight: bold;">dedicated network slices</span> optimized for specific operational requirements. For example, a <span style="font-weight: bold;">logistics company</span> can purchase a <span style="font-weight: bold;">low-latency slice</span> for connected vehicles, a <span style="font-weight: bold;">hospital</span> can deploy a <span style="font-weight: bold;">high-reliability slice</span> for medical telemetry, and a <span style="font-weight: bold;">factory</span> can run an <span style="font-weight: bold;">isolated industrial slice</span> for AGV and automation control. <br /><br />None of these capabilities are fully achievable on <span style="font-weight: bold;">5G NSA (Non-Standalone)</span> architectures because NSA still depends on the underlying <span style="font-weight: bold;">LTE core network</span>. If your <span style="font-weight: bold;">IoT roadmap</span> includes <span style="font-weight: bold;">deterministic SLAs</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">mission-critical telemetry</span>, or <span style="font-weight: bold;">real-time control loops</span> beyond 2027, the carrier’s <span style="font-weight: bold;">5G SA deployment progress</span> is one of the most important metrics to evaluate.</p>
</div>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The pattern is clear. 5G earns its place when latency falls below 30 ms or sustained high bandwidth is required. For everything else, 4G LTE remains a competent network in 2026.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>When 4G Is Still Enough (Defeating the 5G FOMO Narrative)</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A typical US 4G LTE connection in 2026 delivers 30 to 100 Mbps down, 10 to 30 Mbps up, and 50 ms latency. That is enough for:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>All web browsing, email, and social media use.</li>



<li>Music streaming on Spotify, Apple Music, and Tidal at maximum quality.</li>



<li>Video calling on FaceTime, Zoom, Google Meet, and Teams at HD.</li>



<li>Standard and 1080p video streaming on Netflix, YouTube, and Disney+.</li>



<li>Voice calls with VoLTE, which delivers HD audio on 4G.</li>



<li>Most enterprise IoT sensor networks (fleet telematics, smart meters, retail kiosks).</li>



<li>Mobile point of sale and field service tablets.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If your daily use sits in this list, a 5G upgrade is a nice-to-have rather than a need. The biggest 5G benefit for casual users is consistency in dense urban areas during peak hours, where 4G can degrade under load. Outside those windows, the difference is marginal.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5G for IoT: Where 5G RedCap Fits</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most IoT devices do not need 5G. Smart meters, asset trackers, soil sensors, and basic telematics run efficiently on LTE-M or NB-IoT. These low-power, narrowband technologies sip data and last years on a single battery.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G enters the IoT picture for use cases that need more bandwidth or lower latency than LTE-M can deliver.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5G RedCap (Reduced Capability)</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Defined in 3GPP Release 17, RedCap is the 5G New Radio mid-tier. It delivers 100 to 220 Mbps with 10 to 15 ms latency, at lower module cost and lower power than full 5G. RedCap targets:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>HD security and surveillance cameras.</li>



<li>Industrial wearables and AR glasses for field workers.</li>



<li>Retail kiosks and digital signage.</li>



<li>Connected medical devices in hospitals and clinics.</li>



<li>Mid-tier asset trackers that need more than NB-IoT but less than full 5G.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Full 5G (mmWave and high mid-band)</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For industrial robotics, V2X, broadcast-grade video, and private 5G factory networks. Pairs with 5G SA for deterministic latency and network slicing.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For more, see Spenza&#8217;s <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">NB-IoT vs LTE-M vs 5G RedCap guide</a>.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5G Mythbusting in 2026: Battery, Security, and Health</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Three persistent consumer concerns, addressed with current evidence.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Does 5G drain battery faster?</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yes, slightly. Active 5G use draws 10 to 20 percent more battery than equivalent 4G use, mostly because mmWave radios consume more power and 5G modems run at higher clock rates. Modern phones from 2024 onward have closed most of this gap with better power management and Smart Data Mode features. The practical impact for most users is one less hour of mixed-use battery life per day, not a brick by lunchtime.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Is 5G secure?</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G has stronger encryption than 4G. The most important improvement is SUCI (Subscription Concealed Identifier), which replaces the persistent IMSI identifier that 4G devices broadcast in cleartext. This makes it significantly harder to track devices using rogue base stations or IMSI catchers. 5G SA also introduces enhanced authentication (5G-AKA) and end-to-end network slicing isolation. The security picture in 2026 is genuinely better than 4G.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Is 5G safe for human health?</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The WHO, ICNIRP, FCC, and the equivalent regulators in the EU, UK, Japan, and Australia all confirm that 5G radio emissions within regulatory exposure limits are safe. mmWave 5G frequencies are non-ionizing and cannot break molecular bonds. No peer-reviewed evidence has shown harm from compliant 5G exposure as of May 2026.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Conclusion: 5G in 2026 Is Real but Uneven</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G in 2026 delivers measurable improvement over 4G in most US markets. Median speeds run 3 to 6 times higher than 4G LTE. Latency on Standalone 5G has fallen to 20 to 30 ms in real-world conditions, with lab targets below 15 ms. The performance gap will widen as carriers complete 5G SA rollouts and deploy more mid-band spectrum from upcoming FCC auctions (AWS-3 in 2026, upper C-band by 2027).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But 5G is not uniform. mmWave covers blocks. Sub-6 covers cities. Low-band covers states with near-4G speeds. The right tier depends on your use case, not on marketing comfort.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consumers should evaluate <strong>5G coverage</strong>, real-world median speeds, and latency performance in their area before deciding whether the upgrade is worthwhile. For casual users who mainly browse the web, stream HD video, or use social media, today’s <strong>4G LTE networks</strong> still deliver a reliable experience.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Enterprise buyers and IoT product teams face a different challenge. Instead of choosing simply between 4G and 5G, the real decision is selecting the right connectivity tier, whether that is <strong>NB-IoT</strong>, <strong>LTE-M</strong>, <strong>4G LTE</strong>, <strong>5G RedCap</strong>, <strong>mid-band 5G</strong>, <strong>mmWave 5G</strong>, or <strong>private 5G SA</strong>. The long-term goal is building a flexible connectivity stack that can adapt as networks evolve over the next decade.</p>
