<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">For years, global IoT connectivity seemed relatively straightforward.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A company could manufacture connected devices, insert a </span><a href="https://spenza.com/esim/what-is-iot-roaming/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener"><span style="font-weight: 400;">global roaming SIM</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and deploy products almost anywhere in the world. Whether it was a smart meter in Brazil, a fleet tracker in Europe, or an industrial sensor in the Middle East, the assumption was simple: one SIM could power everything globally.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">That model helped accelerate IoT adoption across industries. But in 2026, the landscape looks very different.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Governments are tightening telecom regulations. Operators are paying closer attention to long-term foreign SIM usage. Enterprises are scaling from thousands of devices to millions. At the same time, eSIM technology, SGP.32 orchestration, and </span><a href="https://spenza.com/telecom/iot-device-management/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener"><span style="font-weight: 400;">multi-carrier architectures</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> are changing how organizations think about connectivity itself.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">According to </span><a href="https://www.gsmaintelligence.com/research/iot-market-forecast-to-2035-connections-by-region-and-vertical" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener"><span style="font-weight: 400;">GSMA Intelligence</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the number of IoT connections worldwide is expected to exceed 40.8 billion by the end of the decade, putting enormous pressure on existing connectivity models. As deployments scale globally, the industry is discovering that connectivity is no longer just a technical problem. It is now equally a compliance, orchestration, and operational resilience challenge.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The companies that adapt successfully are no longer treating connectivity as “<strong>just buying SIM cards</strong>.” They are treating it as infrastructure architecture.</span></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><b>What Is Global IoT Connectivity in 2026?</b></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Global IoT connectivity refers to the ability to connect and manage IoT devices across multiple countries, operators, and regulatory environments using cellular networks.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">That sounds simple in theory. In practice, it has become significantly more complicated.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Unlike smartphones, IoT devices often operate unattended for years. A connected medical device deployed in one country may remain active for a decade. A smart utility meter may continuously transmit data without human interaction for its entire lifecycle. A logistics tracker may move across multiple borders every week.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">This creates very different connectivity requirements compared to consumer mobile devices.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Historically, enterprises solved this challenge through permanent roaming. A SIM issued in one country could continuously roam on partner networks across many other countries. For businesses, the benefits were obvious:</span></p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">faster deployments,</span></li>



<li><a href="https://spenza.com/telecom/iot-logistics-supply-chain-connectivity/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener"><span style="font-weight: 400;">simplified logistics</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">,</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">fewer telecom agreements,</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">and easier inventory management.</span></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Instead of negotiating with operators market by market, organizations could scale globally with a single connectivity model.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">But regulators increasingly began questioning this approach.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many governments originally viewed roaming as a temporary service designed for travelers, not as a permanent operating model for millions of industrial devices. As IoT deployments expanded, concerns started emerging around telecom licensing, taxation, network resource usage, lawful interception requirements, and national telecom oversight.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">As a result, the old “one global SIM everywhere” model is gradually becoming less sustainable for large-scale deployments.</span></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Today, global IoT connectivity increasingly depends on a mix of:</span></p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">multi-carrier strategies,</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">localized operator relationships,</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">eSIM and eUICC technologies,</span></li>



<li><span style="font-weight: 400;">and centralized orchestration platforms capable of managing connectivity dynamically across regions.</span></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The goal is no longer simply to connect devices. It is to keep them connected compliantly, reliably, and cost-effectively over many years and across changing regulatory environments.</span></p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Did You Know?</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">Some <span style="font-weight: bold;">enterprise IoT deployments</span> are expected to remain active for more than <span style="font-weight: bold;">10 years</span>. As a result, a connectivity decision made today may still need to remain operational, supported, and compliant well into the <span style="font-weight: bold;">2030s</span>. Long device lifecycles make future-proofing considerations such as carrier flexibility, provisioning standards, network sunsets, and regulatory compliance especially important during the initial design phase.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What Is Permanent Roaming and Why Is It Becoming a Problem?</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To understand why the industry is changing, it helps to first understand what permanent roaming actually means.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Roaming occurs when a SIM uses another operator’s network outside its home country. For example, if a SIM issued in Germany connects to a telecom network in India, the device is roaming.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Originally, roaming was designed for temporary travel. Mobile subscribers traveling internationally would briefly use partner networks abroad before returning to their home networks.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">IoT fundamentally changed that assumption.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of temporary travel, companies began deploying devices that remained active in foreign countries continuously for years. This became known as permanent roaming.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For enterprises, permanent roaming solved several major business problems at once. It reduced deployment complexity, simplified inventory management, accelerated international launches, and removed the need for immediate local telecom agreements.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But as IoT adoption exploded, regulators started asking more questions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Why were millions of foreign SIMs operating permanently inside domestic networks? Were local telecom licensing frameworks being bypassed? Which entities were responsible for lawful interception and telecom oversight? Were foreign operators effectively functioning inside local telecom ecosystems indefinitely?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These concerns became even more significant as IoT deployments expanded into sectors like:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>utilities,</li>



<li>transportation,</li>



<li>healthcare,</li>



<li>smart cities,</li>



<li>and critical infrastructure.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many operators also began viewing large-scale permanent roaming as commercially unbalanced, especially when domestic networks carried long-term traffic for foreign operators.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The 90-Day Rule Explained</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many roaming discussions eventually lead to the so-called “90-day rule.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The exact implementation differs across countries and operators, but the basic concept is simple:<br />if a device continuously roams inside another country for an extended period, it may trigger policy enforcement or compliance review.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That does not necessarily mean immediate disconnection. In practice, enforcement may involve:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>throttling,</li>



<li>traffic deprioritization,</li>



<li>forced profile migration,</li>



<li>or requests for localization.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For enterprises deploying thousands of devices globally, even minor connectivity uncertainty can create significant operational risk.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That is why organizations increasingly view roaming as part of a broader strategy rather than the entire strategy itself.</p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Myth vs Reality</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.8;"><span style="font-weight: bold;">Myth:</span> Roaming is disappearing.<br /><span style="font-weight: bold;">Reality:</span> Roaming still plays a critical role for launches, pilots, and highly mobile deployments. <br /><br /><span style="font-weight: bold;">Myth:</span> eSIM automatically solves compliance.<br /><span style="font-weight: bold;">Reality:</span> Compliance depends on the active operator profile and the telecom regulations of each deployment region. <br /><br /><span style="font-weight: bold;">Myth:</span> One global SIM works forever everywhere.<br /><span style="font-weight: bold;">Reality:</span> Large-scale deployments increasingly require localization strategies to meet carrier, regulatory, performance, and long-term operational requirements.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Which Countries Restrict or Ban Permanent Roaming?</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Permanent roaming regulations have become one of the most important and misunderstood aspects of global IoT deployment planning.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Roaming itself is not disappearing. In fact, it remains essential for many use cases, especially during early deployments or for highly mobile devices. The issue is long-term or indefinite roaming using foreign SIM profiles inside another country.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Different countries approach this issue differently.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some governments openly discourage permanent roaming. Others rely on licensing frameworks, operator enforcement, or telecom oversight mechanisms that make large-scale foreign SIM deployments difficult to sustain over time.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The reasons behind these restrictions vary.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some countries are focused on telecom sovereignty and licensing control. Others want better visibility into devices operating within domestic networks. In some cases, regulators are trying to protect local telecom ecosystems from large-scale dependency on foreign operator identities.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For enterprises, the important point is this:<br />a SIM working technically does not automatically mean the deployment is compliant operationally.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That distinction is becoming increasingly important as IoT projects scale.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A pilot deployment involving a few hundred devices may function without issues. But once an organization deploys tens of thousands of permanently roaming devices inside a regulated market, scrutiny often increases.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is especially relevant for stationary devices such as:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>smart meters,</li>



<li>industrial gateways,</li>



<li>EV charging infrastructure,</li>



<li>kiosks,</li>



<li>and connected retail systems.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In these cases, regulators may question why devices operating permanently inside the country are still using foreign telecom identities years later.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The result is a growing industry shift toward localized operator profiles and <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/esim-orchestration-global-iot/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">eSIM-enabled connectivity orchestration.</a></p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Reality Check</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">Many <span style="font-weight: bold;">enterprise organizations</span> only discover roaming restrictions after they begin scaling deployments internationally. By that stage, replacing or reconfiguring <span style="font-weight: bold;">connectivity infrastructure</span>, updating provisioning strategies, or changing carrier relationships can become significantly more expensive and operationally disruptive. Evaluating roaming policies, localization requirements, and long-term compliance constraints early in the deployment lifecycle can help avoid costly migration projects later.</p>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The Multi-Carrier Strategy Decision Tree</strong></h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="666" height="375" class="wp-image-19869" style="width: 666px;" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Global-Iot-Connectivity-Strategy-1024x576.png" alt="Multi-carrier IoT connectivity strategy decision tree for choosing roaming, eSIM, multi-IMSI, and local operator profiles

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As the industry evolves, enterprises are moving away from single-carrier dependency and adopting multi-carrier connectivity architectures.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is one of the biggest structural shifts happening in global IoT today.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A multi-carrier strategy simply means using different operators, connectivity profiles, or network relationships depending on:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>deployment geography,</li>



<li>compliance requirements,</li>



<li>latency expectations,</li>



<li>and operational goals.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The reason is straightforward: no single operator can optimize every market globally forever.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Relying entirely on one carrier creates several long-term risks. Coverage quality varies by region. Regulatory policies change. Commercial agreements evolve. Outages happen. Roaming enforcement increases. And some countries eventually require localized operator relationships anyway.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A multi-carrier architecture provides flexibility.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example, an enterprise may:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>use roaming during launch,</li>



<li>deploy localized profiles in regulated countries,</li>



<li>use multi-IMSI for cross-border mobility,</li>



<li>and implement local breakout where latency or sovereignty requirements apply.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Increasingly, global IoT connectivity is becoming a layered strategy rather than a single connectivity product.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What makes this transition important is that enterprises are no longer optimizing only for coverage or price. They are focused on building scalable, resilient operations that can support long-term growth and that is a major mindset shift.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to <a href="https://www.juniperresearch.com/press/esim-connections-to-grow-300-per-cent-globally/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Juniper Research</a>, eSIM-enabled IoT connections are expected to grow dramatically over the next several years as enterprises seek more flexible and remotely manageable deployment models. The ability to dynamically localize connectivity without replacing physical SIM cards is becoming increasingly valuable as regulations evolve.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The eSIM-First Global Rollout Playbook</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">eSIM technology is becoming one of the most important building blocks in modern global IoT deployments.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For years, physical SIM management created operational friction at scale. Enterprises often had to maintain multiple regional SKUs, manage inventory across countries, physically replace SIM cards, and coordinate carrier relationships market by market.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At a small scale, this was manageable.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At enterprise scale, it became operationally expensive and difficult to sustain.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An eSIM-first approach changes that model by allowing operator profiles to be remotely provisioned and managed over the air. Instead of replacing hardware physically, organizations can adapt connectivity dynamically throughout the device lifecycle.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That flexibility is increasingly critical in regulated markets.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="666" height="375" class="wp-image-19870" style="width: 666px;" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook-1024x576.png" alt="eSIM-first global IoT rollout workflow showing provisioning, local profile deployment, and compliance orchestration 
" srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook-1024x576.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook-300x169.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook-768x432.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook-1536x864.png 1536w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eSIM-rollout-playbook.png 1672w" sizes="(max-width: 666px) 100vw, 666px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A practical deployment strategy typically follows this approach:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An enterprise may initially launch devices using roaming because it accelerates time-to-market. Once deployments scale, the organization identifies countries where permanent roaming restrictions or localization requirements become relevant. Using eUICC-enabled eSIMs, local operator profiles can then be remotely provisioned where needed.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This dramatically reduces operational disruption compared to replacing physical SIMs manually.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It also helps enterprises avoid long-term SKU sprawl. Instead of manufacturing multiple country-specific hardware variants, companies can maintain a more centralized deployment strategy while still adapting connectivity locally when required.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to multiple telecom industry forecasts, eSIM adoption in enterprise IoT is expected to accelerate significantly between 2026 and 2030 due to growing demand for remote provisioning, operational flexibility, and compliance-aware deployments.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The key shift is this:<br />eSIM has evolved beyond being just a convenience feature. It is increasingly becoming operational infrastructure for global IoT.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How SGP.32 and Multi-IMSI Enable Compliant Global IoT</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As global IoT deployments scale further, enterprises need technologies capable of managing connectivity more intelligently across operators and regions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Two of the most important technologies driving this transition are:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>SGP.32,</li>



<li>and multi-IMSI architectures.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Although often mentioned together, they solve different challenges.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What Is SGP.32?</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://spenza.com/esim/sgp-32-new-esim-standard/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">SGP.32</a> is the GSMA’s Remote SIM Provisioning standard designed specifically for IoT environments.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Earlier eSIM frameworks were largely optimized for consumer smartphone workflows. IoT deployments, however, operate very differently. Devices may remain active for years, operate unattended, and require large-scale remote lifecycle management.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SGP.32 was introduced to better support those enterprise-scale requirements.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The specification enables more scalable orchestration of IoT eSIM profiles, helping enterprises manage connectivity remotely across large device fleets.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In simple terms, SGP.32 helps make IoT eSIM management more operationally practical at scale.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What Is Multi-IMSI?</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://spenza.com/esim/multi-imsi-vs-euicc-guide-iot/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Multi-IMSI technology</a> allows a SIM or eSIM to store multiple network identities associated with different operators.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This helps devices:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>switch network identities dynamically,</li>



<li>improve regional optimization,</li>



<li>increase redundancy,</li>



<li>and reduce dependency on a single operator relationship.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Multi-IMSI is especially valuable for highly mobile deployments such as:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>transportation,</li>



<li>logistics,</li>



<li>connected vehicles,</li>



<li>and international asset tracking.</li>
</ul>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The Most Important Nuance</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the biggest misconceptions in the market is that technologies like eSIM or SGP.32 automatically make deployments compliant.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">They do not.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SGP.32 solves the operational problem of managing profiles at scale. Compliance still depends on:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>which operator profile is active,</li>



<li>local telecom rules,</li>



<li>licensing frameworks,</li>



<li>and roaming regulations within each country.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That distinction is extremely important.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Technology can enable localization. It cannot replace regulatory strategy.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As deployments become more global and regulations continue evolving, enterprises increasingly need orchestration layers capable of managing:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>multiple carriers,</li>



<li>profile lifecycle management,</li>



<li>regional policies,</li>



<li>and compliance-aware connectivity decisions.</li>
</ul>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Cost and Compliance Trade-offs by Strategy</strong></h2>



<figure class="wp-block-image aligncenter size-large is-resized"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="666" height="375" class="wp-image-19871" style="width: 666px;" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince-1024x576.png" alt="Cost vs compliance comparison chart for global IoT connectivity strategies including roaming, multi-IMSI, and local eSIM profiles " srcset="/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince-1024x576.png 1024w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince-300x169.png 300w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince-768x432.png 768w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince-1536x864.png 1536w, /wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Cost-vs-Complaince.png 1672w" sizes="(max-width: 666px) 100vw, 666px" /></figure>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every global IoT connectivity model involves trade-offs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some prioritize operational simplicity. Others prioritize compliance, localization, or long-term scalability.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Roaming-first models remain attractive because they are fast to deploy and operationally simple. For pilots, proof-of-concept deployments, and highly mobile devices, roaming often remains the most practical starting point.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But over time, long-term roaming may introduce:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>compliance uncertainty,</li>



<li>higher operational risk,</li>



<li>and increasing dependency on a single carrier ecosystem.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Localized profile strategies usually improve regulatory alignment and long-term scalability, especially in heavily regulated markets. However, they also introduce greater orchestration complexity.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Multi-carrier architectures enhance resilience and flexibility, but they also increase the need for centralized visibility and more coordinated operations.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That is why the industry is increasingly moving toward hybrid connectivity models.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of asking:“What is the single best global SIM?”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Enterprises are now asking: “What is the best connectivity architecture for each deployment scenario?” That is a fundamentally different way of thinking about global IoT connectivity.</p>
