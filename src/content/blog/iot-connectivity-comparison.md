<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">What Is IoT Connectivity in 2026?</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;"><span style="font-weight: bold;">IoT connectivity in 2026</span> spans four major layers: <span style="font-weight: bold;">cellular connectivity</span> (LTE-M, NB-IoT, Cat-1bis, RedCap, 5G), <span style="font-weight: bold;">LPWAN technologies</span> (LoRaWAN, Sigfox, Wi-Fi HaLow), <span style="font-weight: bold;">short-range protocols</span> (BLE, Zigbee, Matter, Wi-Fi), and <span style="font-weight: bold;">satellite NTN</span>. The optimal connectivity stack depends on factors such as <span style="font-weight: bold;">coverage requirements</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">data consumption</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">power constraints</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold;">expected device lifespan</span>. <br /><br />Modern IoT deployments increasingly combine <span style="font-weight: bold;">multiple connectivity technologies</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">eSIM orchestration</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold;">satellite fallback</span> to improve reliability and global reach. At scale, global IoT connectivity increasingly depends on centralized <span style="font-weight: bold;">IoT Connectivity Management Platforms (CMPs)</span> such as <span style="font-weight: bold;">Spenza</span>, which orchestrate <span style="font-weight: bold;">carrier integrations</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">eSIM provisioning</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">usage policies</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold;">network switching</span> across thousands of connected devices.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The 2026 IoT Connectivity Stack in Numbers</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The IoT market has moved well beyond the &#8220;pilot project&#8221; phase. According to <a href="https://iot-analytics.com/number-connected-iot-devices/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">IoT Analytics</a>, there are now over <strong>21.1 billion connected IoT devices</strong> globally, and that number continues to climb rapidly.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Legacy networks are shutting down faster than many device manufacturers planned. Newer, purpose-built technologies like <strong>5G RedCap</strong> and <strong>satellite NTN</strong> are moving from standards documents into practical commercial deployments. And the <strong>eSIM specification SGP.32</strong>, finalized in 2024, is now seeing accelerated adoption across industrial IoT programs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This means the connectivity decision made today will shape device performance for the next 5 to 10 years. Connectivity planning is now a lifecycle decision, not just a procurement decision.&nbsp;</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The Four Layers of IoT Connectivity</strong>&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Think of IoT connectivity like a toolbox. No single tool handles every job. The right choice depends on the environment, the task, and the constraints of the device doing the work.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>1. Cellular Wide-Area Networks</strong>&nbsp;</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Cellular IoT technologies use licensed spectrum, which means they operate on the same regulated, interference-free bands as your mobile phone. This makes them reliable for devices operating across cities or countries. Technologies like <strong>LTE-M, NB-IoT, Cat-1bis, RedCap, and 5G NR</strong> all sit within this category, although each serves different points on the speed, power, and cost spectrum.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>2. LPWAN Unlicensed Networks</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>LoRaWAN, Sigfox, and Wi-Fi HaLow</strong> operate on unlicensed bands, which keeps costs low but introduces range and interference trade-offs. These are best suited for low-data, battery-powered sensors in environments where cellular coverage is either absent or economically impractical.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>3. Short-Range Connectivity</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>BLE, Zigbee, Thread, Matter, and Wi-Fi 6/7</strong> are proximity technologies. They typically connect devices within buildings or campuses rather than directly to the internet.&nbsp;A smart building, for example, may use Zigbee sensors connected to a cellular gateway that handles the backhaul connection.&nbsp;</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>4. Satellite NTN&nbsp;</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Non-terrestrial network (NTN)</strong> connectivity uses <strong>LEO, MEO, or GEO</strong> satellites to reach devices in locations where ground-based infrastructure simply does not exist. Standardized through 3GPP Release 17, NTN is becoming part of mainstream IoT architecture rather than a niche solution.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The sections below break down where each connectivity technology performs best, where its limitations appear, and how it fits into modern IoT deployments.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Cellular IoT Technologies Explained: LTE-M vs NB-IoT vs RedCap&nbsp;</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Cellular IoT is not a single technology. It is a family of standards, each optimized for a different operational profile. For a technical deep dive, please read <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/nb-iot-vs-lte-m-vs-5g-redcap/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Spenza&#8217;s NB-IoT vs LTE-M vs 5G RedCap breakdown</a>.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>LTE-M</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">LTE-M supports mobility and even voice, which makes it the standard of choice for anything that moves. A connected medical wearable, for example, may travel across cities while continuously transmitting telemetry. LTE-M handles that mobility without losing connectivity.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Typical use cases:</strong> Asset tracking, Connected medical wearables, Fleet management, Healthcare RPM</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>NB-IoT</strong>&nbsp;</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">NB-IoT is built for stationary, low-data devices that need years of battery life. Think of a smart water meter sending one small reading every hour. It does not need high bandwidth, but it does need low power consumption and deep indoor coverage.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Typical use cases:</strong> Smart metering, Parking sensors, Agriculture sensors, Utility infrastructure</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Cat-1bis</strong>&nbsp;</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Cat-1bis is the practical <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/2g-3g-sunset-iot-impact-migration/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">2G and 3G replacement</a> for devices that need a reliable data connection without LTE-M&#8217;s complexity. Unlike older LTE categories, Cat-1bis uses a single antenna design, reducing hardware complexity and lowering certification costs for manufacturers migrating legacy devices.&nbsp;</p>



<div style="background-color: #fff4e5; border-left: 5px solid #FF9800; padding: 1.4em 1.6em; margin: 2em 0; border-radius: 6px; font-family: Arial, sans-serif; box-shadow: 0 2px 5px rgba(0,0,0,0.05);">
<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">Reminder</div>
<p style="margin: 0; line-height: 1.6;">Many <span style="font-weight: bold;">OEMs</span> underestimate <span style="font-weight: bold;">migration timelines</span>. <span style="font-weight: bold;">Module recertification</span> alone can take <span style="font-weight: bold;">6–12 months</span>, depending on the <span style="font-weight: bold;">target region</span>, <span style="font-weight: bold;">carrier requirements</span>, and <span style="font-weight: bold;">device category</span>. Planning migrations early is critical to avoid deployment delays and supply chain disruptions.</p>
</div>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>5G RedCap and eRedCap</strong>&nbsp;</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">5G RedCap (Reduced Capability), introduced in 3GPP Release 17, fills the gap between LPWAN and high-end 5G NR. It is designed for devices that need more bandwidth than LTE-M or NB-IoT, but do not require the cost or power profile of full 5G.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Common examples include:</strong> EV charging stations, Industrial cameras, Mid-tier industrial automation, Smart retail systems</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>eRedCap</strong>, introduced through Release 18, pushes 5G further into constrained IoT deployments with lower power and module costs.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>LPWAN and Short-Range Technologies Still Matter</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Not every IoT problem needs a cellular subscription. Sometimes the most practical solution is a private network or a local mesh.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>When LoRaWAN Makes Sense</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">LoRaWAN shines in environments where a company can deploy its own gateways. A large agricultural operation, an industrial campus, or a smart city pilot can build a private LoRaWAN network and connect thousands of sensors without paying per-device cellular fees. The trade-off is infrastructure ownership and limited bandwidth.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The Decline of Sigfox</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sigfox has faced significant market contraction following financial difficulties and ownership changes. While some regional networks persist, designing new products around Sigfox introduces long-term continuity risk that most product teams are no longer willing to accept.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Where BLE, Zigbee, and Matter Fit</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Short-range protocols are not competitors to cellular. They are complementary. A smart building might use Zigbee sensors throughout each floor, feeding data to a cellular-connected gateway at each entry point.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Matter is emerging as the unifying standard for smart home and commercial building devices, simplifying interoperability across vendors.<br>Understanding how different <a href="https://spenza.com/iot/iot-sensors-types-connectivity/">IoT sensor types connect</a> helps clarify which gateway architecture makes sense for your deployment.</p>

<div style="color: #ff9800; font-size: 1.05em; font-weight: bold; margin-bottom: 0.9em;">When LPWAN Beats Cellular</div>
<ul style="margin: 0; padding-left: 1.2em; line-height: 1.8;">
<li><span style="font-weight: bold;">Isolated campuses</span> or <span style="font-weight: bold;">warehouse deployments</span> with private gateway infrastructure</li>
<li>Sensors transmitting <span style="font-weight: bold;">small payloads</span> only a few times per day</li>
<li><span style="font-weight: bold;">Battery-first device designs</span> targeting <span style="font-weight: bold;">5–10 year operational lifespans</span></li>
<li>Deployments where <span style="font-weight: bold;">per-device cellular connectivity cost</span> exceeds the economic value of the transmitted data</li>
</ul>
</div>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Satellite NTN Is Reshaping Global IoT Coverage</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If cellular IoT is the highway network, <strong>satellite NTN (Non-Terrestrial Network) </strong>is the off-road capability that reaches everywhere the highway does not.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What 3GPP Rel-17 and Rel-18 Changed</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before 3GPP Release 17, satellite connectivity often relied on proprietary systems that were difficult to integrate into standard cellular deployments.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That changed when NTN became part of the standardized cellular ecosystem.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Release 17 introduced standardized NTN support for LTE-M and NB-IoT. Release 18 improved support for constrained devices and low-power satellite communication.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This means IoT devices can increasingly move between terrestrial and satellite connectivity without requiring entirely separate architectures. <a href="https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/technologies/internet-of-things/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">GSMA Intelligence</a> reports that NTN-capable chipsets are already entering commercial supply chains in 2026.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Hybrid Cellular + Satellite Architectures</strong>&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most resilient architecture combines cellular as the primary connection with satellite as an automatic fallback.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A logistics tracker traveling through remote regions may use LTE-M for most of its route, then automatically switch to LEO NTN in coverage gaps.&nbsp;</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Why eSIM Matters for IoT</strong>&nbsp;</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An <strong>embedded SIM (eUICC)</strong> allows cellular profiles to be provisioned, switched, and managed remotely without physical access to the device. For a deployment of 100,000 connected devices across multiple countries, that flexibility is not just a convenience. It is a fundamental operational requirement.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At scale, this typically requires a centralized <strong>Connectivity Management Platform (CMP)</strong> capable of orchestrating provisioning, policy management, and usage visibility across thousands of endpoints. Choosing the right <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/iot-esim-providers/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">eSIM IoT provider</a> is the first decision that determines how well your CMP layer scales.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>What SGP.32 Standardizes</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SGP.32 is the GSMA standard designed specifically to enable eSIM functionality on resource-constrained IoT devices. Earlier eSIM standards assumed devices had screens, user interfaces, and reliable power. SGP.32 removes those assumptions and simplifies remote provisioning for battery-powered and headless devices, making large-scale eSIM orchestration practical for industrial deployments. With adoption accelerating through 2026, it is becoming the foundation for scalable IoT connectivity solutions.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Why Multi-IMSI Matters</strong>&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A multi-IMSI SIM carries multiple network identities within a single physical or embedded SIM. When a device crosses a border or loses signal from its primary carrier, the multi-IMSI logic selects the next best available network automatically.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This reduces dependency on a single operator&#8217;s roaming agreements and maintains coverage resilience across regions.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Important Reminder:</strong> Single-carrier lock-in may reduce short-term complexity, but it can create major operational risk at scale.&nbsp;</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>How to Choose the Right IoT Connectivity Stack</strong>&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Choosing IoT connectivity is really about understanding the deployment context. The best technology depends on five practical questions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>1. Where Will Devices Operate?</strong></p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Urban environments</strong> may work well with NB-IoT or Wi-Fi.</li>



<li><strong>Cross-border deployments</strong> often require LTE-M with multi-IMSI.</li>



<li><strong>Remote infrastructure</strong> with no cellular coverage needs NTN or LoRaWAN for fallback.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>2. How Much Data Will Devices Send?</strong>&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>NB-IoT handles a few <strong>bytes per hour</strong>.&nbsp;</li>



<li>LTE-M handles <strong>kilobytes per minute</strong>.&nbsp;</li>



<li>RedCap handles <strong>video streams</strong>.&nbsp;</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Mismatching data volume to technology is one of the most common and costly design errors in IoT programs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>3. What Are the Power Constraints?</strong>&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Battery-powered devices</strong> running for 10 years need NB-IoT or LoRaWAN&#8217;s power profiles.</li>



<li><strong>Line-powered devices</strong> can use Cat-1bis, LTE-M, or RedCap.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Solar adds an interesting middle ground where burst transmission windows become the design variable.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>4. How Long Will Devices Stay Deployed?&nbsp;</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A temporary campaign deployment has different requirements than a smart meter expected to operate for 10 years. The 2G and 3G sunset timelines make this concrete: any device designed around 2G connectivity today will face forced migration before most product teams expect.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For deployment planning guidance, see <a href="https://spenza.com/iot/iot-devices-buyers-guide/?utm_source=chatgpt.com" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Spenza’s IoT devices buyer’s guide</a>&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>5. Which Pricing Model Fits Best?&nbsp;</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Three models dominate IoT data pricing.&nbsp;</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Per-device</strong> flat fees ($1–5/device/month) work well for predictable, consistent usage.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Pooled plans</strong> distribute a shared data bucket across a fleet, which suits fleets with variable individual consumption.&nbsp;</li>



<li><strong>Per-MB</strong> metered pricing ($0.10–$2/MB) suits very low-volume devices where a flat fee would be wasteful.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For a deeper breakdown of how to structure and optimize these models, see <a href="https://spenza.com/esim/iot-data-plans-cost-optimization/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Spenza&#8217;s guide to IoT data plan cost optimization</a>.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The 2G and 3G Sunset Migration Window</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As discussed earlier, GSMA data confirms 37 operators have retired or are actively retiring 2G, with 39 doing the same for 3G through 2025 and 2026. North America is furthest along. Europe and Asia are following on staggered timelines.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The migration path for most devices runs toward Cat-1bis for simple data applications, LTE-M for mobile applications, and RedCap for higher-bandwidth use cases.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>The 3GPP Roadmap</strong><strong><br></strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Release 17 (2022) introduced RedCap and standardized NTN. Release 18 (2024) brought eRedCap and improved NTN for constrained devices. Release 19, expected to finalize in 2026 and beyond, begins laying the groundwork for 6G and advances NTN further.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For IoT devices with 7–10 year lifespans, the Release 18 and 19 roadmap determines whether today&#8217;s module selection remains viable through the device&#8217;s end of life.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Certification Timelines and Deployment Risk</strong></h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Module certification for new cellular technologies typically takes 6–12 months. Teams that delay migration planning until network sunset notices arrive often face compressed timelines and limited module availability. In practice, evaluations should begin 18–24 months before migration.</p>
