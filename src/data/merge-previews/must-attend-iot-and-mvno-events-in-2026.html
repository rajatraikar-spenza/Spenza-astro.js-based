How do you choose the right audience and meetings?

The right conference is the one where you can meet people who own your unresolved decisions. Start with the deployment or commercial problem, identify the roles needed to address it, then inspect the event’s audience and agenda. A large exhibitor list is less useful than access to the relevant specialist.

For an IoT engineering team, the meeting brief might cover the device model, modem firmware, target countries, installation environment and reporting pattern. Bring the conditions that make your fleet difficult to serve: indoor placement, long sleep periods, intermittent coverage or an expensive field visit. Ask who can evaluate those conditions after the event.

An MVNO team needs a different brief. Set out the target customer, service geography, launch responsibilities and the systems that already exist. The MVNO operating model guide helps define which responsibilities remain with the brand. Use that division of work to decide whether you need a host operator, an enablement partner or a specialist supplier.

Write one decision for each planned meeting. “Understand the platform” is difficult to act on; “confirm who owns failed activation recovery for our proposed device family” gives the supplier a concrete question. Share the brief beforehand and ask whether the attendee can answer it or bring the person who can.

  • Product and engineering: ask for supported device configurations, integration requirements and a route to technical validation.
  • Operations: ask how exceptions are detected, assigned, resolved and recorded across suppliers.
  • Finance and procurement: ask what the quote includes, which charges vary with behaviour and which contractual conditions remain open.
  • Partnerships: ask who owns the next step and what both sides must provide before a commercial decision.

Keep technical vocabulary precise. For example, an “eSIM ready” demonstration does not identify the provisioning architecture or establish support for your fleet. The remote SIM provisioning guide explains why consumer and IoT provisioning require different questions. Ask for the applicable specification, device prerequisites and supported workflow.

Meeting check: Separate what is generally available from a roadmap item, a partner dependency or a demonstration setup. Record the answer with its date and scope so procurement can verify the same claim later.

How do event conversations become procurement evidence?

Event conversations become useful evidence when claims are recorded, verified by the responsible supplier and tested against an agreed deployment scope. Keep the original question, the answer and any conditions together. Assign an owner for the next step so a promising discussion can become a documented technical or commercial decision.

  1. Capture the claim. Record the product, contact, date and conditions. Note whether the answer came from a live demonstration, a document or a verbal explanation.
  2. Request supporting material. Ask for the relevant product documentation, service terms or integration example. Identify anything still dependent on a carrier, partner or future release.
  3. Agree an evaluation. Specify the device, market and workflow to test, including an exception case where useful. Define what a pass or unresolved result means.
  4. Close the decision. Record fit, remaining conditions and the accountable owner. A deferred or rejected option can be a useful outcome if the reason is clear.

For a connectivity management platform, a polished dashboard demonstration should lead to questions about the systems behind it. Ask how delayed usage, partial activation failures and support escalation appear in operational records. The CMP evaluation guide separates SIM and connectivity control from device firmware management, helping you avoid evaluating one product against another product’s responsibilities.

Keep a common record across suppliers. A proposal that states its limits can be more useful than a broad claim with no defined conditions. Share the same fleet assumptions and evaluation criteria, then document where the responses are comparable and where they still need clarification.

Frequently asked questions

Conference planning depends on the edition, audience, pass conditions and the decision your team needs to make. The answers below address common practical questions without assuming that attendance guarantees a result. Verify changing details with the organizer and use supplier documentation to assess any product claim made during the event.

Should an IoT startup attend CES or MWC?

Choose according to the people you need to meet. CES can be relevant to connected products and customer experience, while MWC can support mobile ecosystem and operator conversations. Check the current exhibitor and session lists, identify specific contacts, and compare those opportunities with your actual product and partnership decisions.

Is a free exhibition pass enough?

A free pass may be sufficient for your planned exhibition meetings, but it does not automatically include conference sessions or other access. Read the organizer’s current pass conditions and confirm your intended meetings. Include travel, preparation and follow-up time when assessing the total commitment, even when admission costs nothing.

Can a supplier demonstration replace a pilot?

A demonstration shows behaviour under the conditions chosen for that demonstration. It does not establish performance on your devices, networks or installation sites. Ask for the configuration and limitations, then agree which claims need testing against your own deployment scope before making a procurement or rollout decision.

How should a small team divide event responsibilities?

Assign ownership by the decisions being explored rather than by booth count. One person may own technical evidence, another commercial terms and another partner follow-up, with roles combined where necessary. Use a shared brief and record so the team can compare answers without repeating the same introductory conversation.

Are recordings available after a completed conference?

Availability depends on the organizer, session and pass conditions. Check the event’s official materials or contact the organizer rather than assuming every talk was recorded. If a recording is unavailable, ask the speaker for published slides or documentation and verify whether the information still describes the current product.

How can we measure whether attendance was worthwhile?

Start with your own costs and intended decisions. Record which meetings produced verified evidence, resolved a requirement or created an accountable next step. Track later commercial outcomes separately and avoid attributing them to the event without a supporting record. A contact count alone does not demonstrate return on investment.
